OpenTallas · Technical Report

OpenTallas: Hardwiring Language Models for Order-of-Magnitude Faster Tokens

Mask-ROM weights beside the multipliers, each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and a decode dataflow whose every block is sized from a per-token budget give one user 8,910 tokens/s on Qwen3-8B at 8K context from one reticle, within 0.3% of the floor set by streaming its KV cache, and 5,448 / 4,978 tokens/s on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 200K / 1M context from an array of shippable two-die packages — 4.4–6.5× the same cores streaming their weights from HBM. These are model rates (RTL-calibrated for Qwen3-8B, specification model for V4.1); reduced versions of both models decode bit-exactly in RTL, and the redesigned blocks are passing their per-block benches.

1Abstract

Interactive and agentic use of language models is gated by per-user decode speed, which batching does not improve. We find that decode is dominated by data movement, and that the costliest data to move are the weights. Once the weights stop moving, a token becomes a latency problem — the critical path of one layer times the number of layers — plus, at long context, a KV-bandwidth problem. We present OpenTallas, a model-specific architecture that stores weights in mask ROM beside the multiply-accumulate lanes, keeps each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and executes decode as a compiled dataflow of fixed-latency units whose widths and depths are derived top-down from a per-token budget. We build it for a small interactive model (Qwen3-8B on one reticle, 8K context) and a large sparse long-context model (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on 188 reticle dies in shippable two-die packages, 200K and 1M context). At batch 1 the Qwen3-8B core reaches 8,910 tokens/s per user in its RTL-calibrated model at specification widths — above its 8,494 target and within 0.3% of the 8,941 floor set by streaming the FP8 KV cache, so KV bandwidth, not the dependency chain, now limits it — and 13,613 with DFlash speculation on three lane copies (specification model); that is 6.5× the same core streaming its weights from six HBM stacks and 41× a calibrated H200 on one reticle of logic; and 5,448 / 4,978 tokens/s for V4.1 at 200K / 1M (11,950 / 10,383 with its native MTP), 4.7× / 4.4× an HBM array of equal logic area. Keeping bit-exactness at these rates required a new arithmetic contract: the reference's sequential sums alone cap V4.1 near 1,180 tokens/s at any width. These are model rates, not silicon: the RTL decodes reduced versions of both models bit-exactly and the redesigned blocks meet their per-block benches, but whole-core token simulation and routing of the redesigned cores are pending; the calibrated Qwen3 core went from 386 times its budget to 5% under it in five rounds of levers. On the same core, weights in ROM use 15.9× less energy per step than weights from HBM (ASAP7 sign-off).

2Introduction

A growing class of language-model workloads is limited by how fast one user's tokens arrive, not by how many tokens a data centre can produce. Interactive System-1 models sit in a conversation or a control loop. Human turn-taking leaves gaps of about 200 ms across languages [Stivers], and full-duplex speech models are built to answer within that budget (Moshi reports 200 ms in practice [Moshi]); voice agents such as GPT-Live [GPT-Live] and Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models [TML] hold the conversation on a fast model for exactly this reason. In-car and home assistants inherit the same conversational budget [citation needed], and in robotics and driving the budget is set by physics: fast-slow systems run a large reasoning model at about 5 Hz beside a 20 Hz control loop because the large model cannot keep up with the vehicle [Fast-slow VLA].

Background System-2 models reason before they act, so their wall-clock time is the length of the reasoning trace times the time per output token. Computer-use agents today take tens of minutes on OSWorld tasks that take a person a few minutes, and most of that time is large-model calls [OSWorld-Human]. Background speed also becomes foreground latency: generative user interfaces are designed and coded per request, and Google reports its implementation "can sometimes take a minute or more" [Generative UI]; self-improving software agents iterate write–test–evaluate loops whose duration is the sum of their generated tokens [AlphaEvolve].

Serving systems and hardware roadmaps, however, optimise cost per token. Continuous batching and paged KV caches [vLLM] maximise tokens per second per accelerator, and batching amortises weight reads across users without making any one stream faster. Public trackers such as Artificial Analysis report per-user output speed by model and over time [Artificial Analysis]; whether the output speed of frontier models is improving or declining is not something we could verify from that source [citation needed]. What is clear from first principles (§4) and from our own measurement is that one GPU decodes an 8B model for one user at a few hundred tokens per second: 94 tokens/s at BF16 and 151 at FP8 on a Blackwell workstation GPU we measured, and 217 on an H200 at FP8, calibrated to NVIDIA's own measurements.

The payoff. Figure 2-1 previews the result at the design points we chose for each class: Qwen3-8B at 8K context, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 200K and 1M. When weights live in mask ROM beside the multipliers and every block is sized from a per-token budget, one user gets 4.4–6.5× the tokens per second of the same decode core streaming its weights from HBM, and about 40× a single-die GPU at equal logic silicon on Qwen3-8B. The rates come from the RTL-calibrated timing model (Qwen3-8B) and from executable budget models priced with routed RTL depths (V4.1); the RTL behind them decodes bit-exactly on reduced vehicles, and §8.5 reconciles the analytical model, the calibrated model and the RTL as built.

Two objections follow immediately, and we address them early rather than late. Is it more expensive? Per token served it is not: on the same decode core, keeping weights in ROM uses 15.9× less energy per step than streaming them from HBM (measured); in the specification model the V4.1 array spends 18.5 mJ per token against 1,509 mJ for the HBM array at 200K, provided idle pipeline stages are clock-gated; and aggregate throughput at equal silicon is higher, not lower (§8.7). The mask-set cost is amortised over the model's deployment (§10.1). Why build silicon for one model? Because the interactive layer of a paired system changes slowly, frontier architectures are converging, and everything except the ROM contents is reused across models (§10.2).

Our thesis is that when a model is fixed, both its weights and its schedule can be hardware. Mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of silicon area, and a schedule compiled before fabrication replaces run-time synchronisation with sequencing measured in cycles. What is left is latency: the critical path of each layer, times the number of layers, plus the time to stream each user's KV cache. The design problem therefore becomes a budgeting problem — derive from a per-token target how wide and how shallow every block must be, keep every operator exact, and check each block against its budget before the whole core is built. The approach has real costs, a mask set per model and capacity that scales with die count, which we treat as first-class constraints (§5, §10).

This report makes the following contributions:

  • The measured insight that decode energy is data movement, not arithmetic, and that moving weights is its largest part: on one core, weights from HBM cost 15.9× the energy of weights in ROM (§5.1, §8.4).
  • A characterisation of fast per-user decode as four challenges — the memory wall for weights and, at long context, KV; the dependency chain that remains once weights are free; multi-die latency; and irregular exact operators — each tied to a design principle (§4, §5).
  • A top-down design method — target, binding resource, budget, per-block requirements, microarchitecture, per-block performance benches — with executable budgets for both workloads, and an honest three-layer reconciliation of the analytical model, the RTL-calibrated model and the RTL as built (§6.3, §8.5).
  • An arithmetic contract (chunks of eight, summed by a pairwise tree) that keeps every operator bit-exact against a golden model while removing the sequential sums that alone would cap V4.1 near 1,180 tokens/s (§6.5).
  • Redesigned decode cores for both models — a 1,024-lane vector stream unit, K-split attention, dedicated weight, attention and indexer engines, a streaming exact top-512 select, a per-bank Engram gather — with KV in attached HBM behind a refresh-aware controller, and lane copies measured at 528 µm² per lane that make speculation pay on ROM (§6.4–§6.8).
  • An evaluation at the design points: iso-area Qwen3-8B against single-die GPUs and the same core on HBM; V4.1 at 200K and 1M on shippable packaging; speculative decoding with acceptance measured on eleven workloads; batching from 1 to 1,024 users with energy; and the power each design must meet (§8).
  • Implementation evidence on ASAP7: routed units, memory compilers with self-test and repair, scan and ATPG, a hierarchical full-chip flow and an OpenAI-compatible host interface (§7).

3Background

3.1 Anatomy of one decode token

A decoder-only model produces one token per step. Each of its L layers applies, in order: a normalisation; projections of the hidden vector into queries, keys and values (matrix-vector products against the layer's weights); attention of the new query over every stored key and value of this user's context (the KV cache), appending the new key and value; an output projection; a second normalisation; and a feed-forward block (dense, or a mixture of experts of which a router picks a few). A final normalisation and a vocabulary projection produce logits, and the chosen token becomes the next step's input.

  • Every step depends on the one before. A matrix-vector product needs the whole previous vector; softmax needs every score; the next token needs every logit. A token is a chain of on the order of ten dependent steps per layer.
  • At batch 1 each weight is used once per token. Two FLOPs per weight read, so the work is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound.
  • Per-user state grows with context. The KV cache is read every step; its size per token decides whether long context is a bandwidth problem of its own.

3.2 Representative workloads

Deployed systems increasingly pair two classes of model. A small, fast "System-1" model holds the interaction: it must answer within human conversational latency. A large "System-2" model does deeper work in the background and is delegated to when needed. OpenAI's GPT-Live (July 2026) keeps continuous voice interaction on a low-latency model and delegates search, reasoning and agentic work to GPT-5.5 running in the background [GPT-Live]. Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models (May 2026) state the same split: "when a task requires deeper reasoning than can be produced instantaneously, the interaction model delegates to a background model that runs asynchronously" [TML]. Both classes are latency-bound: the first for the user's turn, the second because agentic loops chain many calls.

We therefore pick one representative of each class rather than the models most favourable to ROM:

  • System-1: Qwen3-8B [Qwen3], a widely used dense GQA model of the size class used for fast interaction. It is also small enough to test the single-die case (C3).
  • System-2: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a large sparse mixture of experts (552 B total, 16 B active) with compressed KV for a 1 M-token context. It carries the architectural line frontier open models are following for long context: latent/compressed KV (MLA, DeepSeek-V2 [DS-V2]), sparse attention selected by a learned indexer (DeepSeek-V3.2 [DS-V3.2]), compressed sparse attention to one million tokens (DeepSeek-V4 [DS-V4]), plus manifold-constrained hyper-connections with a Sinkhorn projection [mHC] and Engram conditional-memory lookup [Engram]. If future frontier models follow this line, their decode will look like V4.1's: small active weights, fixed attention work per token, context-dependent cost confined to an indexer, and many irregular operators (C4).

Selection criteria, applied to every profiled candidate. (1) Class: is it a representative System-1 or System-2 model? (2) Openness: released weights and configuration, so a bit-exact golden can be written. (3) Per-user state: does per-token state traffic stay small at long context, or does it dominate regardless of where weights live? (4) Architectural novelty relative to the other target. (5) ROM capacity at the analytical model's density.

What the graphs imply for hardware.

  • Qwen3-8B is shallow and wide: 20 dependent stages per layer in the specification's count, dominated by the matrix passes. With weights in ROM its token time is set by the chain of those stages and, at 8K, by streaming each user's 604 MB FP8 KV cache (§4.2).
  • V4.1 fixes its attention work per token at 640 rows, whatever the context, and moves the context-dependent cost into the indexer on 8 layers. Its MoE touches only 6 of 384 experts, and a ROM machine, like an HBM one, reads only their rows; with speculation or batching it reads the union of the experts the positions choose (§8.6).
  • V4.1 needs five unit types beyond the matrix engine and stream unit: FP8/MXFP4 block-dot lanes, SELECT (top-k, router top-6, candidate blocks), a Sinkhorn unit, the Engram hash-and-gather unit, and the IEEE divide/sqrt/softplus functions. Its dependency chain per layer is about twice Qwen's, and in an array each layer adds about five collectives between dies.

4Motivation

4.1 Why speed, not cost

The workloads of §2 each carry a latency budget set by a person or by physics, and in every case the budget is spent mostly on decode. Table 4-1 collects them. The common thread is that a faster token cannot be bought with more accelerators: batching and data parallelism raise aggregate throughput, while one stream's rate is bounded by the per-token critical path. Cost per token is therefore the wrong objective for these workloads; time per output token for one user is the right one, subject to a cost per token that is not worse (§10.1).

The third appears once a model spans many dies; the fourth comes from the structure of modern models themselves. 4.2 Challenge 1 — The memory wall sets the per-user ceiling: for weights always, for KV at long context

Every token touches every active weight and each user's whole KV cache, so where they live — their bandwidth and their capacity together — bounds single-user speed.

At batch 1 each weight is used for exactly one multiply-accumulate per token, so decode performs about one floating-point operation per byte of weight read. An accelerator that keeps the weights in HBM therefore runs at the speed of its memory interface, not its arithmetic: the per-user rate cannot exceed memory bandwidth divided by the bytes a token must read. Batching raises aggregate throughput by reusing each read across users, but it leaves the rate of any one user where it was.

The ceiling is low for both workload classes. For the System-1 model it lies in the hundreds of tokens per second even on the fastest current HBM part, and a sparse System-2 model does not escape it: its active weights per token are comparable to a dense 8B model's (Figure 4-1). Moving the weights next to the arithmetic changes the picture qualitatively, because on-die memory bandwidth grows with the number of memory macros rather than with the number of package pins.

The KV cache is the second wall, and it does not move into ROM. A Qwen3-8B token at 8K context reads each user's whole cache once: 1.21 GB at BF16, or 604 MB at FP8 (Table 4-3). Streamed from six HBM3E stacks at 5.4 TB/s sustained, the FP8 cache alone takes 112 µs — 122,881 cycles, more than twice the 57,740-cycle sweep of the weights over 131,072 lanes — so even a machine whose weights cost nothing to read cannot exceed about 8,900 tokens/s per user at 8K, and a BF16 cache halves that. DeepSeek-V4.1 compresses its attention state to a fixed 13.9 MB per token at any context, but its lightning indexer scores every compressed key: 38.5 MB of index keys and 2.3 G multiply-accumulates per token at 200K, and 182.7 MB and 11.0 G at 1M, where the indexer's arithmetic exceeds that of the six routed experts (8.5 G). In V4.1, context enters the token only through the indexer (docs/ARCH_SPEC_V41.md §2).

The difficulty on the weight side is capacity. On-die storage must hold the whole model, and the densities differ by an order of magnitude between memory technologies (Table 4-2). We find that even the System-1 model does not fit one reticle at BF16; a one-die product needs about 4-bit weights, as the only published hardwired product also uses [HC1]. SRAM-only storage would need about a dozen wafers for the System-2 model. Per-user state competes for the same area: with an 8K cache in on-die SRAM, the Qwen3-8B reticle cannot also hold its 4-bit weights (224.6 mm² of ROM needed, 120.2 mm² left; results/roofline/iso_area/qwen3_8b.json), so on both ROM designs each user's cache lives in HBM beside the die (§6.7).

4.3 Challenge 2 — Once weights are free, a token is a dependency chain

When weights no longer bound the rate, a token's time is the critical path of one layer times the number of layers, and on a GPU each dependent boundary costs on the order of a microsecond.

A decode layer is a chain: each matrix-vector product needs the whole output of the previous step, softmax needs every score, and the next token needs every logit. We count 20 dependent stages in a Qwen3-8B layer, so a token is 720 stages long; a V4.1 layer is about twice as deep and adds collectives between dies. Every cycle a stage exposes is paid 720 times on Qwen3-8B. With the weights swept at the rate the lanes allow (57,740 cycles), the Qwen3-8B target leaves 38,804 cycles for exposed latency: at most 54 cycles per dependent stage, fewer than one fast exponential pipeline takes (49). For V4.1 on the published packaging, 176 of the 225 µs of a token (78%) is dependent latency — a compute chain of 117 µs, 40 µs of collective latency over about 210 on-path collectives, 12 µs of pipeline hops and 7 µs of control — and only 43 µs is the weight sweep (Figure 8-9).

We measured the cost of one dependent boundary on a Blackwell GPU (Figure 4-2). The key finding is that the expensive part is not kernel launch, which programmatic dependent launch and persistent megakernels already remove, but the physics of the dependency itself: a result produced by every streaming multiprocessor must pass through the L2 cache, and a memory fence must wait for L2's acknowledgement. The cheapest all-SM boundary that actually delivers the activation vector costs about a microsecond, and none of the alternative designs we tried — data-carried flags, per-producer flags, cluster hierarchies, or Megatron-style operator pairing — was faster. Only dependencies that stay inside one SM or one cluster are cheap, and a matrix-vector product spread over the whole chip cannot stay there.

A machine with a fixed model can avoid the problem rather than optimise it: when every dependency is known at build time, a sequencer can issue the next dependent operation in a cycle or a few and start a consumer as soon as its producer has written enough elements. But that only helps if every block is also built to a latency budget. Our first decode core, designed bottom-up on reduced vehicles, took 49.9 M cycles per 8K token at shipped shape in its calibrated model — 386 times its budget. At 2K the causes are plain: the one-element-per-cycle stream unit was busy for 8.7 M cycles, attention's P·V, not split across the lane groups, for 4.7 M, and full-unit barriers stalled issue for 13.5 M (results/arch/qwen3_budget.json baseline_as_built; §8.5).

Implication. The schedule must be resolved at build time, and every block must be sized top-down so that each dependent stage exposes only its budgeted cycles.

4.4 Challenge 3 — Scaling past one die must not add latency

Frontier models span many dies, so communication, collectives and packaging enter the critical path of every token.

A System-2 model needs tens of reticle dies (Table 4-2). Splitting it adds two kinds of serial cost: pipeline hops, where a token's activations move from the dies holding one group of layers to the next, and collectives, where the dies of a tensor group combine partial results inside a layer. Both are latency-bound, because decode activations are kilobytes, not megabytes. Keeping every die's weights local and moving only activations keeps the bytes small, but the latency does not shrink with them: on shippable packaging a V4.1 token spends a fifth to a quarter of its time on links (Table 4-4), and the collective latency after the last partial sum is the largest single fixed term of the token.

Packaging is a hard constraint rather than a parameter. High-density die-to-die links reach only adjacent dies, board links pay forward-error-correction latency, and interposers are limited in size: four reticle-sized dies with their HBM exceed the 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production in 2026 [TSMC 2026], and the one announced four-die product was reportedly cut back to two [Rubin Ultra]. The packaging choice is therefore evaluated, not assumed (§6.9).

4.5 Challenge 4 — Modern models are irregular, and hardwiring must stay exact and fast

MoE routing, top-k indexers, Sinkhorn-normalised hyper-connections, Engram lookups and sparse attention must become fixed-latency pipelines at about 1 GHz while matching the reference bit for bit — and the reference's own summation order is part of the latency.

The System-2 line of models adds operators that are neither matrix products nor element-wise functions, and each sits on the critical path (Table 4-5). They are data-dependent selections, iterative normalisations and hashed lookups — exactly the operations a conventional datapath implements with loops, sorts and serial reducers. Built that way they cost from a few percent of the per-user rate (a two-pass top-512: 2% at 200K, 9% at 1M) to most of it (an 8-lane stream unit: 1,482 instead of 5,448 tokens/s); each needs its own pipeline, sized to its share of the budget.

The same operators make exactness hard. Floating-point addition is not associative, so a different reduction order is a different model, and faults in selection or indexing produce fluent but wrong tokens. The full-scale compiler lane found three such faults only by comparing against the reference token by token (results/abi3/deepseek_v41_pool_and_decode_path.json). Worse, the reference fixes an order that hardware cannot make fast: the released golden sums sequentially — 2,560 terms in a hyper-connection mix, 2,048 in wo_a, 640 in p·v — and a dependent FP32 chain of n terms takes at least n adder latencies however wide the engine is, 12,800 cycles for the longest at a 5-cycle add. Under those orders the fixed part of a V4.1 token is 838 µs at 200K, which caps it near 1,183 tokens/s at any width (§6.5). Finally, every unit must close timing near 1 GHz on a 7 nm-class process, where a single combinational binary32 addition already takes several nanoseconds.

5Key insights and design principles

5.1 Key insight: data movement, not arithmetic, dominates decode — and weights are the costliest data to move

The first insight concerns energy and bandwidth together. At batch 1 a decode step performs one multiply-accumulate per weight it reads, so the cost of a step is set by how far each weight travels, not by the arithmetic done on it. Architecture work has long observed that moving an operand costs more than operating on it, and recent industry analysis puts data movement at over 80% of the energy of a large AI cluster [He 2026]. Decode is an extreme case of that rule (Challenge 1): its arithmetic intensity is about one operation per byte, so almost all of its energy and time is data movement, and the largest single stream is the weights.

We measure this directly on the same decode core (§8.4). With weights streamed from HBM, moving them is 83% of the step's energy and 88% of the gap to the ROM design; with weights in ROM beside the lanes, reading them falls to about 1% of the step, and what remains is dominated by on-chip movement — the clock tree and registers — rather than arithmetic, which is at most 28% even under an upper-bound activity model (Figure 8-5). Two consequences follow. The right place for weights is where they are consumed, which motivates P1; and once weights are local, the next levers are on-chip movement (clock gating, register count) and the per-user state that still streams from HBM, not the arithmetic units.

5.2 Central insight: when the model is fixed, weights and schedule can be hardware

The key insight is that the two limits of §4.2 and §4.3 share a cause: a general-purpose accelerator must treat both the weights and the schedule as data, fetched and synchronised at run time. For a model that will be served unchanged for months, both can be fixed at fabrication. Weights then become mask ROM placed beside the multipliers, and the schedule becomes a compiled program whose dependencies are resolved before the chip is built.

This removes the memory wall for weights rather than moving it. Each ROM macro delivers a word per cycle to the lanes next to it, so aggregate weight bandwidth grows with silicon area, not with package pins. A ROM bit is also denser than an SRAM bit and far cheaper to read than an HBM access (Table 5-1), which is what lets a System-1 model fit one reticle and a System-2 model fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. With the weights no longer re-read per user, a single user keeps the lanes busy: batching becomes a way to add aggregate throughput, not the price of efficiency.

The insight has costs that bound where it applies. Each model and version needs its own mask set; capacity scales with die count; the KV cache is per-user state and lives in HBM beside the ROM die, so its bandwidth is untouched by ROM; and long-context attention traffic therefore bounds a ROM design exactly as it bounds an HBM one, which is why the workload's state per token matters (§3.2, Table 4-3).

5.3 Third insight: once weights are free, latency must be budgeted, not discovered

Removing the weight stream does not make a token fast by itself; it exposes everything else. The first hardwired core we built bottom-up — each unit designed and optimised on a reduced vehicle, then scaled — decoded the reduced model bit-exactly, yet its calibrated model took 386 times its per-token budget at Qwen3-8B's shipped shape, and the V4.1 core was 150 times slower at 200K than the analytical model it was meant to realise (§8.5). Nothing in it was wrong; every unit simply had the width and depth that was convenient, not the width and depth the token needed. The analytical model that promised thousands of tokens per second had assumed widths, chaining and reduction orders the RTL did not have.

The lesson is the one accelerator vendors apply: start from a per-token target at a stated context and batch, identify the binding resource, and give every other block a budget — lanes, bytes per cycle, elements per cycle and, above all, the latency each dependent stage may expose. A block is then built and verified against its own requirement with a performance bench, and the whole core is simulated and routed only after the blocks meet their budgets (§6.3). The same discipline exposes requirements the models had hidden: a latency floor that leaves only a quarter of the V4.1 token for all block widths, a golden summation order that makes the target unreachable, and a power limit that the signed-off multiplier exceeds (§6.5, §6.8).

6.3 Top-down design method (P6)

We design each core from a per-token target downwards. The target and the model graph fix the binding resource; the budget gives every other resource a share of the token; each block gets a requirement and a performance bench that checks it; whole-core simulation and place-and-route come last.

The method replaced a bottom-up one that had produced a correct but slow core (§5.3). Figure 6-5 shows the steps with the value each took for the two workloads, and Table 6-11 the resulting budgets. Both budgets are executable models committed with tests: tools/arch_budget_qwen3.py (results/arch/qwen3_budget.json) and tools/arch_budget_v41.py (results/arch/arch_budget_v41.json); the Qwen3 test replays the program the core runs, at the shipped shapes and 8K, through the RTL-calibrated sequencer model against a ratchet that falls as each block lands and ends at the budget target.

Choosing the target. For Qwen3-8B two resources bind on the reticle: the weight sweep through 131,072 lanes (57,740 cycles, a ceiling of 19,027 tokens/s) and the KV stream from six HBM3E stacks. The target is the larger of the sweep over 55% of the token and the KV stream over 95% of it: at 8K with FP8 KV the stream binds, giving 129,348 cycles or 8,494 tokens/s per user at 1.0986 GHz, the slowest routed Qwen3 token-path clock (4,247 with a BF16 cache; at 2K the sweep binds at 10,465). A dependent FP32 chain of n terms cannot finish in fewer than n adder latencies, however wide the engine: 12,800 cycles for the 2,560-term mix at a 5-cycle add. Under the golden orders the fixed part of a 200K token is 838 µs, four times the whole 204 µs target, and the best any width can do is 1,183 tokens/s (1,187 at 8K, 1,160 at 1M). The contract that removes this is the golden's own split_sum with the chunk length fixed instead of the chunk count:

  • every accumulation is cut into contiguous chunks of at most 8 terms, each summed sequentially from +0, and the chunk sums are added by a pairwise tree padded with +0;
  • a tree level that crosses dies is the one-shot collective's fixed rank order;
  • where both operands are FP8/FP4 with 32-element block scales, the chunk is the quantisation block: its dot is formed exactly and rounded once, which is what makes a block-dot lane cost about 78.5 µm² per multiply-accumulate instead of a full FP32 adder's;
  • the golden, the ISA and the RTL change together, and bit-exactness stays defined against the golden.

Status and numerics. The V4.1 golden carries the contract as a mode (HDC_V41_ARITH=chunk8, selectable per operator class so re-specified units can be brought up one class at a time), and the ISA-level simulator is bit-exact with it in every logit at position 7 and over a prompt plus four generated tokens. Against the legacy order the logits differ by at most 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ up to position 7; at position 8 a routed-expert selection flips at a near-tie of the reduced vehicle's 12-expert router, and later differences follow such flips. Neither order is closer to the oracle (the vendor model in BF16 on a GPU): legacy matches 3 of 16 teacher-forced steps and chunk-8 matches 2, with mean oracle-token gaps of 0.345 and 0.341, and both reproduce the oracle's first token with the same margin (results/arch/v41_arith_contract.json). The Qwen3 vector stream unit's reducer implements the same contract. A second revision (R-ARITH v2: the normalisation's reciprocal root folded into the consuming matrix products' outputs, online softmax in 64-entry blocks) is committed as a further golden mode and is required by the first dependency-chain lever.

Exactness costs nothing once the order is chosen for hardware; a sequential order written for a reference implementation costs 78% of the token.

On both ROM designs each user's KV cache — and on V4.1 its compressed rows and index keys — lives in HBM stacks attached to the ROM die, never in on-die SRAM; SRAM holds buffers and staging only. In on-die SRAM, an 8K cache left the Qwen3 reticle no room for its own 4-bit weights (§4.2), and the V4.1 array had carried an 8,192-token admission limit; in HBM, capacity is no longer the limit: six 24 GB stacks hold 201 users at 8K with FP8 KV on the Qwen3 reticle, and five stacks per V4.1 die hold 6,272 users at 200K and 1,203 at 1M (93.5 MB per user on the busiest die). Prefetch. The Qwen3 cache for layer l + 1 (positions below the current token, which do not depend on it) streams while layer l computes, into a 33.6 MB two-layer buffer; the token's own K/V row stays on die and is written back behind the stream. Index keys stay in the model's own FP4 codes with one UE8M0 scale per 32 elements — 68 bytes per key, lossless because the golden quantises every key to FP4 before use, and 3.8× smaller than BF16.

The controller is a design problem, not a given. A stream at 90% of raw peak must survive DRAM refresh. The HBM comparator's refresh study measured three things on the RTL (reduced vehicles, every run bit-exact; Table 6-20). All-bank refresh alone costs about 9% of raw peak, so it just meets a 90% floor on the Qwen3 stream. A controller with a shallow queue lets one refreshing pseudo-channel stall an in-order stream for about the refresh time: with 64-beat queues, all-bank refresh fell to 0.852 at 8 pseudo-channels and cost up to 15% of a V4.1 token on the comparator vehicle. And the best refresh policy depends on the access pattern: a refresh-aware per-bank scheduler that refreshes the not-yet-refreshed bank the fewest queued bursts need reaches 0.965–0.993, but on the indexer's long sequential key scan its tie-break must favour the most recently activated bank (0.956 of peak per stack) rather than the closed-bank-first choice that suits weight streams (0.851).

6.8 Lane copies, area and power

A lane copy is a multiply-accumulate lane that shares its group's weight word, so m copies let one weight read serve m positions — speculative slots or batched users — but never speed a single position. Routed at 528 µm² per lane, two copies cost the Qwen3 reticle 138 mm², paid for by the SRAM the KV cache vacated. Power, not area, then bounds the reticle: at the signed-off MAC energy the die exceeds its cooling limit.

Lane copies. ot_hdc_lane_copy is 16 lanes of the exact BF16 multiplier and the circulating FP32 adder with its interleave registers, sharing the group's weight word, split tree, result port and argmax. It routed on ASAP7 at 8,449 µm², closed at a 0.9 ns target with 67 ps of setup slack (about 1.2 GHz): 528.08 µm² per lane, against 1,071.8 µm² for a whole matrix-engine lane (results/physical_abi3/asap7/hdc/ot_hdc_lane_copy/physical.json). One copy of all 131,072 lanes is therefore 69.2 mm². On the V4.1 die the MTP design point uses m = 2, which doubles every engine and the block area from 87 to 174 mm²; m = 3 buys 12% more for 50% more area, and m = 4 would exceed the 329 mm² envelope.

6.9 Multi-die fabric and packaging (C3)

Multi-die systems keep every die's weights local and move only activations (P4). A tensor group of four dies spans a pair of shippable two-die packages; its fixed-order collectives return bit-identical results on every die; between tensor groups, a pipeline of layer groups passes one token's state. Link latency after the last partial sum, not bandwidth, is what remains on the critical path.

Forty layers, each an attention half and a MoE half joined by hyper-connections (a four-copy residual stream). 6.11 Host interface and runtime

7.4 Hierarchical full-chip implementation

A hierarchical flow characterises each block's ports, derives timing budgets from the tile's paths, the floorplan distances and a wire model fitted to routed links (0.60 ps/µm + 190 ps), hardens blocks against their budgets with LEF/Liberty abstracts, and assembles tiles and reduced 2×2 dies with post-route timing at every block pin. Mesh ports use a pipelined credit link so every tile pin is a register. Its lessons are now physical rules for every new block: every block boundary is registered, inputs and outputs (a registered 512-bit output needs about 300 ps inside the block, with the router's clock-to-pin about 270 ps); clock insertion on the matrix engine takes about 540 ps; repair needs a 40% slew margin and a 15 ps setup margin for estimated against extracted wire delay; and routes carry a real hold margin.

Sequencing. Following the top-down method (§6.3), whole-core token simulation and place-and-route of the as-built cores were stopped once the budgets showed their cycle counts were far from the target: an Fmax sweep, DFT and clock-gating runs, the full-chip and V4.1 core routes, and several unit routes and performance campaigns on the as-built core would have characterised a core that is being replaced. The analytical model was right about the destination and silent about the route. DeepSeek-V4.1: a dependency-chain-bound token

On the published graph a V4.1 token is 176 of its 225 µs dependent latency (78%): a compute chain of 117.0 µs, collective latency of 40.3 µs over about 210 on-path collectives, 11.9 µs of pipeline hops and 7.2 µs of control, with only 42.8 µs of weight sweep. The baseline links take the collectives to 26.4 µs, and the specification's widths take the weight sweep to 27.5 µs, but its 183.6 µs token is still 61% compute chain. Three levers, priced in order on the same widths and adopted as integration requirements, attack the chain directly:

  1. Reductions off the critical path (−23 µs). An RMSNorm's reciprocal root scales the next matrix product's outputs instead of its input, so the sum of squares and the root run beside the weight sweep; softmax becomes online, exponentials streaming behind the scores with a running-maximum rescale in the release kernel's 64-row blocks. Both are contract changes (R-ARITH v2, §6.5).
  2. Chaining for every unit (−16 µs). Matrix products, the attention and indexer scans and the local selects start on their producer's first tile or row instead of its last element; the weight sweep on the path falls from 23 to 13 µs because each product now overlaps its producer.
  3. Shorter stages (−13 µs). The vector unit's measured depths: stream base 21 cycles (29 in the graph), reducer tail 25 (32), rsqrt 58 (90), √softplus 161 (259).

Together they reach 131.5 µs, 7,602 tokens/s per user at 200K (7,715 at 8K, 7,138 at 1M) on the same 87 mm² of blocks, and 15,818 with MTP. What they leave is instructive: 75.5 µs of compute chain, 26.4 µs of collective latency — now a fifth of the token and the largest single fixed term — 14.3 µs of weight sweep, 9.1 µs of hops and 4.1 µs of control. A fourth lever, keeping attention and its projections on the two dies of one package to halve the attention collectives, was measured and not adopted: at the specification's widths it loses (7,304 against 7,602 at 200K; 6,451 against 7,138 at 1M) because it doubles each die's attention and indexer work, and even with those engines doubled it gains only 2–3% over spending the same area without the move, and loses 4% at 1M. The next lever on the collective term is in-network reduction or a shorter pair link, not a different placement.

8.6 Speculative decoding: a gift for HBM, earned on ROM with lane copies

Speculative decoding is a cure for the memory wall. On an HBM machine a verify pass rides on one read of the weights and the speed-up approaches the acceptance length: 3.6× on the Qwen3-8B comparator. On a ROM machine a verify pass costs its positions in multiply-accumulates, so speculation pays only when lane copies let one weight read serve several positions: with none it gains nothing; with three, DFlash gives the Qwen3-8B reticle 1.77× and carries it past its KV floor, because the verify slots share one read of the cache; with two, MTP gives the V4.1 array 2.2×. Routed experts break the rule on both machines: six positions touch 34.6 distinct experts, not six.

How speculation works. A drafter proposes a block of B − 1 tokens; the target scores all B positions in one pass and keeps the longest prefix that matches its own greedy choice, plus one token of its own. Under greedy decoding the output is identical to plain decoding, token for token; our golden, ISA and RTL keep it bit-exact because every verify position uses the same fixed-order arithmetic as a one-position step (§6.5). The speed-up is the accepted length per pass, τ, divided by the cost of a pass relative to a plain step. For Qwen3-8B we use DFlash [DFlash], a 1.05 B-parameter block-diffusion drafter that fills a whole block in one parallel pass; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ships its own drafter, DSpark (three stages over a block of five) [DSpark].

Acceptance is a property of the workload

We measured τ on the real Qwen3-8B with the released DFlash drafter on eleven workloads (Table 8-18). The spread is the finding: thinking-mode reasoning, the System-2 use of §2, accepts 3.4–3.9 tokens per pass, less than half the 8.2 we measure on the same MATH-500 prompts with thinking off — which reproduces the paper's 8.01 — and agentic workloads range from 2.6 (web navigation) to 11.3 (JSON). We therefore price every speculative figure at the pooled τ = 4.1 (561 blocks on the real model in an FP32 reference) and treat the paper's 6.5–8.0 only as a sensitivity. For V4.1 no acceptance has been measured on the real model; τ = 4.1 is a decision, bracketed by 3.27–3.80 (derived from a published survival curve) and about 5 (reported for SGLang with DSpark) [vLLM DSpark, LMSYS DSpark].

The two machines respond differently

Speculation trades arithmetic for memory traffic: a verify pass does B positions of multiply-accumulates for one read of each weight. An HBM machine decoding one user is bandwidth-bound, so its multipliers idle while weights stream; the extra positions ride on the same read and the pass costs little more than a plain step. A hardwired ROM machine has already removed that bottleneck: each lane consumes one weight per cycle, so a B-position pass costs about B times the lane time and the gain collapses to about τ / B. Lane copies change this: with m copies sharing each weight word (§6.8), a pass costs ⌈B/m⌉ sweeps, and the best block is about m. And on the Qwen3-8B reticle a second effect appears: the verify slots also share one read of each user's KV cache, so speculation divides the KV stream per token by the tokens per pass — the only lever that takes the reticle past its KV floor.

8.7 Batching: per-user rate, throughput and energy

Batching does not make a ROM design faster per user, and it fills the two designs differently. The Qwen3-8B reticle is KV-bound from the second user: its total throughput is flat at 8,941 tokens/s from batch 2, a ceiling the same core on HBM approaches only near 128 users. The V4.1 array is a 28-stage pipeline: each user keeps the batch-1 rate up to 28 users, and aggregate throughput rises to 442 K tokens/s at 1,024 users at 200K, four times the HBM array, while energy per token falls as the stages fill.

Qwen3-8B at 8K

On the reticle, users share the chain's latency and the weight words but each has its own KV stream, so a step of b users costs max(latency + ⌈b/m⌉ × weight sweep + b × attention, b × KV stream). From the second user the KV stream binds: total throughput is flat at 8,941 tokens/s — 5.4 TB/s over 604 MB — and per-user rate falls as 1/b. Lane copies do not help here, because each user's KV is its own. The same core on HBM starts at 1,379 tokens/s and rises with batch as weight reads are shared, reaching 8,573 at 128 users; it never passes the ROM's total within its KV capacity (195 users), and the single-die GPUs stay at 3,468 (H200, FP8) and 3,532 (B200 NVFP4 per die) at 128.

8.9 Design-space exploration: every rejected alternative as an ablation

Every alternative we evaluated either lowered the per-user rate or kept it at the cost of a harder build; none was rejected on taste. In the specification model the largest single effects are the reduction order, the stream-unit width and the engines' widths; the wafer is rejected on aggregate throughput, HBM attach and manufacturing, not on batch-1 latency.

Wafer-scale integration

What it was. The V4.1 package logic laid out as reticle fields stitched on one wafer, so a die-to-die hop becomes an on-wafer field crossing, with KV in HBM stacks around the wafer edge, compared with the array at equal silicon (docs/WAFER_VS_ARRAY_ISO_AREA.md; both priced by the pre-specification critical-path model, the array on shipping two-die packages).

What we found. On a published stitched mesh (125 ns per 28.55 mm field crossing) the wafer is slower at batch 1, 0.81–0.94 of the array, because collectives across its many fields dominate. With a designed express fabric the batch-1 comparison becomes a near tie: the wafer matches the array whenever a crossing costs about 56–71 ns or less, our routed express link crosses a field in about 30 ns, and at 25 ns the wafer leads by 3–11% with the built divider (up to 15% at the wire limit). An earlier edition that priced the array on idealised links had the wafer losing at batch 1, and we then gave batch-1 latency as the reason to reject it; with realistic links that reason no longer holds. The wafer loses instead on aggregate throughput — from three wafers up the array delivers 1.33–1.65× the wafer's aggregate at 4,096 users under every fabric, because at equal silicon it carries 6.5× the HBM stacks — on HBM attach, since a wafer's perimeter gives 43 stacks against 5 per 815 mm² die and must also carry the wafer-to-wafer SerDes, and on manufacturing: in-place defect tolerance instead of discarding bad dies, cross-field stitching, an express long-wire fabric measured only as one link, and whole-wafer test. The manufacturing comparison is qualitative — no yield or cost model exists for either machine — but every item except the array's HBM count per package is harder for the wafer.

9Related work

GPU decode. Serving stacks capture decode in CUDA graphs and fuse element-wise work into neighbouring kernels; Programmatic Dependent Launch overlaps a kernel's prologue with its predecessor. Megakernels run a forward pass as one persistent kernel whose instructions wait on counters in memory: Hazy Research's Llama-1B megakernel [Hazy] and the Mirage Persistent Kernel [MPK]. They remove launch overhead but keep the weight stream (C1) and the L2 round trips of cross-SM dependencies (C2), which we measure directly. Vendor-measured concurrency-1 rates [NIM, GPUStack] calibrate our GPU rows; our own measurement shows that batch-1 efficiency against the roofline varies by nearly 2× across stacks (§8.4).

Communication overlap. Fused kernels overlap tensor-parallel communication with computation on GPUs, hiding up to 96% of it for GEMMs [FLUX] and speeding communication-heavy sublayers by 30% on average [T3]; TensorRT-LLM fuses the all-reduce with the following RMSNorm for DeepSeek on B200 [TRT-LLM], and in-fabric reductions cut the latency of small collectives [NVLink SHARP]. We adopt the overlap for the bytes, and find that in decode the latency after the last partial sum remains on the critical path.

Speculative decoding. Draft-and-verify decoding is lossless under greedy and rejection sampling [Leviathan]. Drafters range from feature-level heads (EAGLE-3 [EAGLE-3]) and built-in multi-token prediction (DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-V3], DSpark on DeepSeek-V4 [DSpark]) to block-diffusion drafters that fill a block in one pass (DFlash [DFlash]); serving engines report DSpark's acceptance only as bounds or verify-window lengths [vLLM DSpark, LMSYS DSpark]. All of them were designed for bandwidth-bound GPUs; we show that their benefit depends on the machine's binding resource, that on a ROM machine it returns only with lane copies that share a weight read, and that the acceptance on thinking-mode workloads is half the published figure (§8.6).

On-chip-SRAM dataflow machines. Groq's tensor streaming processor makes execution deterministic by removing reactive hardware (arbiters, caches) and scheduling everything in the compiler [TSP], a principle we share (P2, P5). Cerebras keeps weights in on-wafer SRAM (vendor-reported 44 GB on 46,225 mm² for WSE-3 [WSE-3]). Both trade capacity for bandwidth with SRAM; ROM's density advantage is what lets a large MoE fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. SambaNova (tiered SRAM/HBM/DDR reconfigurable dataflow), Tenstorrent (mesh of programmable cores) and d-Matrix (SRAM digital in-memory compute) occupy other points of the same trade-off [citation needed].

Model-specific silicon. Etched builds a transformer-specific datapath that keeps weights in HBM [citation needed]. Taalas HC1 hardwires Llama-3.1-8B on one 815 mm² TSMC 6 nm die and reports about 17,000 tokens/s per user [HC1], the closest prior work and our calibration anchor. We differ in targeting a large sparse System-2 model across dies at 200K and 1M context, in keeping per-user state in attached HBM, in designing top-down from a per-token budget with a published arithmetic contract and bit-exact verification chain, and in comparing against the same core on HBM at equal logic area.

Packaging. Two-die packages on interposers of about 3.3 reticles are in volume production; TSMC reports 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production and larger classes to come [TSMC 2026, CoWoS], and the one announced four-die GPU was reportedly cut back to two dies [Rubin Ultra]. Board-reach 112G PAM4 links pay 100 ns or more for KP4 FEC [UCIe PHY, IEEE 802.3df]; the OIF CEI-112G agreements and UALink allow lighter FEC [OIF CEI-112G, UALink], which is the basis of our baseline's package link.

10Discussion

10.1 Economics: is it more expensive?

We find that per token served a hardwired machine is cheaper to run at the batch sizes where per-user speed matters, and that its fixed cost is a question of deployment volume. The primary evidence is measured: on the same decode core and the same step, weights in mask ROM use 15.9× less energy than weights streamed from HBM, with 88% of the gap in moving weights (§8.4). That comparison holds the logic fixed, so it isolates the memory technology; its absolute values are ASAP7-predictive. The specification models extend it to the design points (Table 10-1): at batch 1 the Qwen3-8B reticle spends 4.4× less system energy per token than the same core on HBM, and the V4.1 array 81.6× less than the HBM array at 200K — if idle pipeline stages are clock-gated. The advantage narrows with batch on Qwen3-8B, because per-user KV traffic, not weights, dominates both machines' energy by 64 users (§8.7), and it holds on V4.1, where the array's energy per token falls with batch as well.

Throughput per silicon is not the price of speed either. The Qwen3-8B reticle's total throughput from two users on (8,941 tokens/s, KV-bound) is above what the same core on HBM reaches at 128 users (8,573), and the V4.1 array's saturated aggregate is 3.96× the HBM array's at equal logic area. The analytical model's older tokens-per-joule ratios against the B200 (9.04× for Qwen3-8B, 12.33× for V4.1) assumed 0.33 pJ per MAC against a measured 3.97 on the matrix engine and 16.5 on the whole step; they remain superseded.

The fixed cost is the mask set and design. Leading-edge mask sets are estimated in the tens of millions of dollars and complete designs at 7 nm at $50–75 M [SemiAnalysis]. Because the ROM is via-programmed, a new model or version changes the via layers and the ROM contents, not the logic (docs/MEMORY_COMPILERS_AND_BIST.md), so the per-model cost is a partial mask set [citation needed for its price] on an otherwise reused design. The fixed cost is amortised over the tokens served in the model's deployment [citation needed for typical volumes], and latency has value of its own for the workloads of Table 4-1, which a cost-per-token metric does not capture.

10.2 Why build silicon for one model?

Three observations make a model-specific chip reasonable. First, the paired System-1/System-2 structure separates a slowly changing interaction layer from a replaceable background model; OpenAI describes the background model of GPT-Live as swappable without replacing the real-time layer [GPT-Live], so the model that most needs speed is also the one that changes least. Second, frontier open architectures are converging: latent or compressed KV, learned sparse attention and mixtures of experts recur across successive generations [DS-V2, DS-V3.2, DS-V4], so the units a model like V4.1 needs are likely to be needed again. Third, only the ROM contents are model-specific: the vector stream unit and arithmetic contract are shared between the Qwen3 and V4.1 cores, and the unit library, the top-down budget tools, the verification chain and the physical flow carry over.

The costs are real. A model cannot be updated in the field; a new version needs new via masks and a new die run; capacity is fixed at fabrication; and a model whose value lies in frequent retraining is a poor fit. Deployment lifetimes of production models are not well documented publicly [citation needed], and the economics of §10.1 depend on them.

10.3 Best achievable today, and what comes after

The baseline adopts only levers with a published basis for shipping now. A lever sweep on the pre-specification graph, each priced against plain option (b) at 200K, bounds what else is reachable (results/roofline/critical_path/best_achievable_today.json): the light-FEC link alone is +8% and overlapped collectives +2%, together +10% (the baseline); hiding half of the package-crossing latency as well, the best case reported for fused GPU kernels, would add to +12% but no shipping system hides the latency of a small dependent decode all-reduce, so it is not counted; pairing the two packages as one substrate module is +21% but is roadmap packaging; a tensor group of two in each package (option a) is −7% per user and +15% aggregate; and a denser ROM that put a layer on one package's two dies would need 60.5 Mb/mm² against 44.4 today — essentially Taalas HC1's whole-die average, with no public 5–7 nm mask-ROM cell to support it — and would speed nothing, because the tensor group sets the split. Beyond the baseline the specification names its own next levers: in-network reduction for the 26 µs collective term; doubling the attention, indexer and weight engines (+8% at 200K for 70 mm², inside the envelope) if silicon falls short; and, for Qwen3-8B, the KV format, which is now the rate limit at 8K, and a lower-energy lane, which is the power limit.

10.4 Limitations

The headline rates are models, not silicon. The Qwen3-8B rate is the RTL-calibrated timing model — fitted within a few cycles to the RTL on the reduced vehicle — run at the specification's widths on the real program, and it assumes an FP8 KV streamer that is not yet built; the V4.1 rates are the specification model, verified on the program by an event replay, with two of its blocks (the vector unit and the attention engine) still without benches. The clocks are the slowest routed units, not closed cores. The per-user rates are calibrated only against one self-reported product; memory macros are model-grade views on a predictive PDK; the GPU boundary costs are measured on a workstation Blackwell (GB202), not a B200; and V4.1's acceptance length is a decision, not a measurement. ROM removes weight traffic, not per-user state traffic, so models whose state grows with context (Table 3-1) gain less, and at long context the ROM ÷ HBM advantage shrinks (Table 8-12).

11Conclusion

Fast per-user decode is limited on today's accelerators by weight bandwidth, by the synchronisation of a long dependency chain, and at long context by the bandwidth of each user's KV cache. Fixing the model in silicon removes the first and turns the second into a design problem: mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of area, and a compiled schedule of fixed-latency units turns synchronisation into a few cycles — but only if every unit is built to a per-token budget. Designing top-down from that budget, with an arithmetic contract chosen for hardware and per-user state in attached HBM, we took the calibrated Qwen3-8B core from 386 times its budget to within 0.3% of its KV floor at 8,910 tokens/s at 8K, and specified a DeepSeek-V4.1 array of shippable packages at 5,448 and 4,978 tokens/s at 200K and 1M, 7,602 with its dependency-chain levers, and more than twice that with native speculation on lane copies. What remains to settle is the whole-core simulation and routing of the redesigned cores, the FP8 KV streamer, a multiply-accumulate lane within the power budget, and the accuracy of the quantised formats; each is marked pending with the record that will settle it.
