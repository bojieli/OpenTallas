OpenTallas: Hardwiring Language Models for Order-of-Magnitude Faster Tokens

Mask-ROM weights beside the multipliers, each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and a decode dataflow whose every block is sized from a per-token budget project 8,910 tokens/s for one Qwen3-8B user at 8K context on one reticle, within 0.3% of the ideal KV bandwidth ceiling before integrated streaming overhead, and 8,622 tokens/s on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at 1M context (9,041 at 200K) from a one-rack array of shippable two-die packages — 2.4–6.5× model-specific HBM comparators under matched logic-area budgets, each at its best modeled configuration. These are model rates (RTL-calibrated for Qwen3-8B, specification model for V4.1) on link and power values checked against published standards and datasheets; reduced versions of both models decode bit-exactly in RTL, and the adopted blocks are passing their per-block benches.

Design study · 27 September 2026 · Evidence classes and source records are defined in §8.1. Physical estimates use ASAP7, a predictive 7 nm process.

1Abstract

Interactive and agentic use of language models is gated by per-user decode speed, which batching does not improve. We find that decode is dominated by data movement, and that the costliest data to move are the weights. Once the weights stop moving, a token becomes a latency problem — the critical path of one layer times the number of layers — plus, at long context, a KV-bandwidth problem. We present OpenTallas, a model-specific architecture that stores weights in mask ROM beside the multiply-accumulate lanes, keeps each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and executes decode as a compiled dataflow of fixed-latency units whose widths and depths are derived top-down from a per-token budget. We build it for a small interactive model (Qwen3-8B on one reticle, 8K context) and a large sparse long-context model (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on 188 reticle dies in shippable two-die packages, 1M context primary and 200K secondary). At batch 1 the RTL-calibrated model projects 8,910 tokens/s per Qwen3-8B user at specification widths — above its 8,494 target and within 0.3% of the ideal 8,941-token/s FP8 KV bandwidth ceiling. Its 123,301-cycle dependency chain is slightly longer than the 122,881-cycle KV stream floor; whether they overlap with bounded buffers remains unverified — and 13,613 with DFlash speculation on three lane copies (specification model); that is 6.5× the same core streaming its weights from six HBM stacks; and, for V4.1 at 1M, 8,622 tokens/s in the adopted design (23,578 with its native MTP at an acceptance of 5.0; 9,041 and 24,602 at 200K), 2.4× an HBM array of equal logic area in that array's best configuration on shipping hardware — a 96-die tensor group in one NVL72-class switched domain — and 2.0× with MTP on both, conditional on collective overlap and the 1.087 GHz system clock, neither yet demonstrated on the whole system; at the wall, with static power and switches charged to both machines, its energy per token is 10–15× lower at a filled 28-user pipeline and 3.8–4.2× lower at batch 1. The compiled schedule's synchronisation advantage is an on-chip property — a dependent handoff costs 5 cycles in RTL against 371 ns measured on a GB202 GPU — while across packages both machines pay link physics and the modeled advantage narrows to 8–13× the best measured GB200 collective. Keeping bit-exactness at these rates required a new arithmetic contract: the reference's sequential sums alone cap V4.1 near 1,180 tokens/s at any width. These are model rates, not silicon: the RTL decodes reduced versions of both models bit-exactly and the adopted blocks meet their per-block benches, but whole-core token simulation and routing are pending. Under assumed production energy inputs the Qwen3-8B reticle spends 82 mJ per token at batch 1, most of it streaming its KV cache; a common-HBM-KV reduced-step cross-check gives a derived 5.7× energy ratio for ROM over HBM weight storage.

2Introduction

A growing class of language-model workloads is limited by how fast one user's tokens arrive, not by how many tokens a data centre can produce. Interactive System-1 models sit in a conversation or a control loop. Human turn-taking leaves gaps of about 200 ms across languages [Stivers], and full-duplex speech models are built to answer within that budget (Moshi reports 200 ms in practice [Moshi]); voice agents such as GPT-Live [GPT-Live] and Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models [TML] hold the conversation on a fast model for exactly this reason. In-car and home assistants inherit the same conversational budget [citation needed], and in robotics and driving the budget is set by physics: fast-slow systems run a large reasoning model at about 5 Hz beside a 20 Hz control loop because the large model cannot keep up with the vehicle [Fast-slow VLA].

Background System-2 models reason before they act, so their wall-clock time is the length of the reasoning trace times the time per output token. Computer-use agents today take tens of minutes on OSWorld tasks that take a person a few minutes, and most of that time is large-model calls [OSWorld-Human]. Background speed also becomes foreground latency: generative user interfaces are designed and coded per request, and Google reports its implementation "can sometimes take a minute or more" [Generative UI]; self-improving software agents iterate write–test–evaluate loops whose duration is the sum of their generated tokens [AlphaEvolve].

Serving systems and hardware roadmaps, however, optimise cost per token. Continuous batching and paged KV caches [vLLM] maximise tokens per second per accelerator, and batching amortises weight reads across users without making any one stream faster. Public trackers such as Artificial Analysis report per-user output speed by model and over time [Artificial Analysis]; whether the output speed of frontier models is improving or declining is not something we could verify from that source [citation needed]. What is clear from first principles (§4) and from our own measurement is that one GPU decodes an 8B model for one user at a few hundred tokens per second: 94 tokens/s at BF16 and 151 at FP8 on a Blackwell workstation GPU we measured, and 217 on an H200 at FP8, calibrated to NVIDIA's own measurements.

Two deployment questions follow. What is the energy cost? With a common HBM KV path, the reduced Qwen3 step uses 5.7× less derived energy with ROM weights than with HBM weights (ASAP7 routed logic plus modeled memory bytes). Under assumed production energy inputs the Qwen3-8B reticle spends 82 mJ per token at batch 1 against 541 mJ for the same core on HBM at the same 3.5-bit weights (6.6×), most of the ROM's share being the KV stream in its own HBM stacks. The V4.1 array at 1M spends 2.34 J per token at the wall at batch 1 — static power and switches charged to both machines — against 8.83 J for the best HBM comparator, and 128 mJ against 1.30 J at a filled 28-user pipeline, and falls further as the pipeline fills (§8.8); its modeled aggregate throughput at equal silicon rises with that fill (§8.7); and because the schedule is fixed, its saturated worst case bounds its power, so the whole rack is provisioned at 58.3 kW against 132 kW for a GB200 NVL72. Mask-set amortization depends on deployment volume and remains unquantified (§10.1). Why build silicon for one model? Because the interactive layer of a paired system changes slowly, frontier architectures are converging, and everything except the ROM contents is reused across models (§10.2).

Our thesis is that when a model is fixed, both its weights and its schedule can be hardware. Mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of silicon area, and a schedule compiled before fabrication replaces run-time synchronisation with sequencing measured in cycles. What is left is latency: the critical path of each layer, times the number of layers, plus the time to stream each user's KV cache. The design problem therefore becomes a budgeting problem — derive from a per-token target how wide and how shallow every block must be, keep every operator exact, and check each block against its budget before the whole core is built. The approach has real costs, a mask set per model and capacity that scales with die count, which we treat as first-class constraints (§5, §10).

This paper makes the following contributions:

  • The source-pinned energy cross-check that decode energy is data movement, not arithmetic, and that moving weights is its largest part: on a common-KV reduced step, HBM weights cost 5.7× the derived energy of ROM weights (§5.1, §8.4).
  • A characterisation of fast per-user decode as four challenges — the memory wall for weights and, at long context, KV; the dependency chain that remains once weights are free; multi-die latency; and irregular exact operators — each tied to a design principle (§4, §5).
  • A top-down design method — target, binding resource, budget, per-block requirements, microarchitecture, per-block performance benches — with executable budgets for both workloads, and source-pinned evaluation of the adopted designs with their evidence limits (§6.3, §8.5).
  • An arithmetic contract (chunks of eight, summed by a pairwise tree) that keeps every operator bit-exact against a golden model while removing the sequential sums that alone would cap V4.1 near 1,180 tokens/s (§6.5).
  • Decode cores for both models — a 1,024-lane vector stream unit, K-split attention, dedicated weight, attention and indexer engines, a streaming exact top-512 select, a per-bank Engram gather — with KV in attached HBM behind a refresh-aware controller, and lane copies measured at 528 µm² per lane that make speculation pay on ROM (§6.4–§6.8).
  • An evaluation at the design points: iso-area Qwen3-8B against single-die GPUs and the same core on HBM; V4.1 at 200K and 1M on shippable packaging; speculative decoding with acceptance measured on eleven workloads; batching from 1 to 1,024 users with energy; synchronisation cost against a GB200 NVL72; and the power each design draws and must be provisioned for, on link and power values checked against normative sources and datasheets (§8).
  • Implementation evidence on ASAP7: routed units, memory compilers with self-test and repair, scan and ATPG, a hierarchical full-chip flow and an OpenAI-compatible host interface (§7).

3Background

3.1 Anatomy of one decode token

A decoder-only model produces one token per step. Each of its L layers applies, in order: a normalisation; projections of the hidden vector into queries, keys and values (matrix-vector products against the layer's weights); attention of the new query over every stored key and value of this user's context (the KV cache), appending the new key and value; an output projection; a second normalisation; and a feed-forward block (dense, or a mixture of experts of which a router picks a few). A final normalisation and a vocabulary projection produce logits, and the chosen token becomes the next step's input.

  • Every step depends on the one before. A matrix-vector product needs the whole previous vector; softmax needs every score; the next token needs every logit. A token is a chain of on the order of ten dependent steps per layer.
  • At batch 1 each weight is used once per token. Two FLOPs per weight read, so the work is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound.
  • Per-user state grows with context. The KV cache is read every step; its size per token decides whether long context is a bandwidth problem of its own.

3.2 Representative workloads

Deployed systems increasingly pair two classes of model. A small, fast "System-1" model holds the interaction: it must answer within human conversational latency. A large "System-2" model does deeper work in the background and is delegated to when needed. OpenAI's GPT-Live (July 2026) keeps continuous voice interaction on a low-latency model and delegates search, reasoning and agentic work to GPT-5.5 running in the background [GPT-Live]. Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models (May 2026) state the same split: "when a task requires deeper reasoning than can be produced instantaneously, the interaction model delegates to a background model that runs asynchronously" [TML]. Both classes are latency-bound: the first for the user's turn, the second because agentic loops chain many calls.

We therefore pick one representative of each class rather than the models most favourable to ROM:

  • System-1: Qwen3-8B [Qwen3], a widely used dense GQA model of the size class used for fast interaction. It is also small enough to test the single-die case (C3).
  • System-2: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a large sparse mixture of experts (552 B total, 16 B active) with compressed KV for a 1 M-token context. It carries the architectural line frontier open models are following for long context: latent/compressed KV (MLA, DeepSeek-V2 [DS-V2]), sparse attention selected by a learned indexer (DeepSeek-V3.2 [DS-V3.2]), compressed sparse attention to one million tokens (DeepSeek-V4 [DS-V4]), plus manifold-constrained hyper-connections with a Sinkhorn projection [mHC] and Engram conditional-memory lookup [Engram]. If future frontier models follow this line, their decode will look like V4.1's: small active weights, fixed attention work per token, context-dependent cost confined to an indexer, and many irregular operators (C4).

Selection criteria, applied to every profiled candidate. (1) Class: is it a representative System-1 or System-2 model? (2) Openness: released weights and configuration, so a bit-exact golden can be written. (3) Per-user state: does per-token state traffic stay small at long context, or does it dominate regardless of where weights live? (4) Architectural novelty relative to the other target. (5) ROM capacity at the analytical model's density.

What the graphs imply for hardware.

  • Qwen3-8B is shallow and wide: 20 dependent stages per layer in the specification's count, dominated by the matrix passes. With weights in ROM its token time is set by the chain of those stages and, at 8K, by streaming each user's 604 MB FP8 KV cache (§4.2).
  • V4.1 fixes its attention work per token at 640 rows, whatever the context, and moves the context-dependent cost into the indexer on 8 layers. Its MoE touches only 6 of 384 experts, and a ROM machine, like an HBM one, reads only their rows; with speculation or batching it reads the union of the experts the positions choose (§8.6).
  • V4.1 needs five unit types beyond the matrix engine and stream unit: FP8/MXFP4 block-dot lanes, SELECT (top-k, router top-6, candidate blocks), a Sinkhorn unit, the Engram hash-and-gather unit, and the IEEE divide/sqrt/softplus functions. Its dependency chain per layer is about twice Qwen's, and in an array each layer adds about five collectives between dies.

4Motivation

4.1 Why speed, not cost

The workloads of §2 each carry a latency budget set by a person or by physics, and in every case the budget is spent mostly on decode. Table 4-1 collects them. The common thread is that a faster token cannot be bought with more accelerators: batching and data parallelism raise aggregate throughput, while one stream's rate is bounded by the per-token critical path. Cost per token is therefore the wrong objective for these workloads; time per output token for one user is the right one, subject to a cost per token that is not worse (§10.1).

Against that objective we identify four challenges that together bound per-user decode speed. The first two apply to any single device: where weights and per-user state live, and the chain of dependent steps that remains once they are local. The third appears once a model spans many dies; the fourth comes from the structure of modern models themselves. 4.2 Challenge 1 — The memory wall sets the per-user ceiling: for weights always, for KV at long context

Every token touches every active weight and each user's whole KV cache, so where they live — their bandwidth and their capacity together — bounds single-user speed.

At batch 1 each weight is used for exactly one multiply-accumulate per token, so decode performs about one floating-point operation per byte of weight read. An accelerator that keeps the weights in HBM therefore runs at the speed of its memory interface, not its arithmetic: the per-user rate cannot exceed memory bandwidth divided by the bytes a token must read. Batching raises aggregate throughput by reusing each read across users, but it leaves the rate of any one user where it was.

The ceiling is low for both workload classes. For the System-1 model it lies in the hundreds of tokens per second even on the fastest current HBM part, and a sparse System-2 model does not escape it: its active weights per token are comparable to a dense 8B model's (Figure 4-1). Moving the weights next to the arithmetic changes the picture qualitatively, because on-die memory bandwidth grows with the number of memory macros rather than with the number of package pins.

The KV cache is the second wall, and it does not move into ROM. A Qwen3-8B token at 8K context reads each user's whole cache once: 1.21 GB at BF16, or 604 MB at FP8 (Table 4-3). Streamed from six HBM3E stacks at 5.4 TB/s sustained, the FP8 cache alone takes 112 µs — 122,881 cycles, more than twice the 57,740-cycle sweep of the weights over 131,072 lanes — so even a machine whose weights cost nothing to read cannot exceed about 8,900 tokens/s per user at 8K, and a BF16 cache halves that. DeepSeek-V4.1 compresses its attention state to a fixed 13.9 MB per token at any context, but its lightning indexer scores every compressed key: 38.5 MB of index keys and 2.3 G multiply-accumulates per token at 200K, and 182.7 MB and 11.0 G at 1M, where the indexer's arithmetic exceeds that of the six routed experts (8.5 G). In V4.1, context enters the token only through the indexer (docs/ARCH_SPEC_V41.md §2).

The difficulty on the weight side is capacity. On-die storage must hold the whole model, and the densities differ by an order of magnitude between memory technologies (Table 4-2). We find that even the System-1 model does not fit one reticle at BF16; a one-die product needs about 4-bit weights, as the only published hardwired product also uses [HC1]. SRAM-only storage would need about a dozen wafers for the System-2 model. Per-user state competes for the same area: with an 8K cache in on-die SRAM, the Qwen3-8B reticle cannot also hold its 4-bit weights (224.6 mm² of ROM needed, 120.2 mm² left; results/roofline/iso_area/qwen3_8b.json), so on both ROM designs each user's cache lives in HBM beside the die (§6.7).

4.3 Challenge 2 — Once weights are free, a token is a dependency chain

When weights no longer bound the rate, a token's time is the critical path of one layer times the number of layers, and on a GPU each dependent boundary costs on the order of a microsecond.

A decode layer is a chain: each matrix-vector product needs the whole output of the previous step, softmax needs every score, and the next token needs every logit. We count 20 dependent stages in a Qwen3-8B layer, so a token is 720 stages long; a V4.1 layer is about twice as deep and adds collectives between dies. Every cycle a stage exposes is paid 720 times on Qwen3-8B. With the weights swept at the rate the lanes allow (57,740 cycles), the Qwen3-8B target leaves 38,804 cycles for exposed latency: at most 54 cycles per dependent stage, fewer than one fast exponential pipeline takes (49). For V4.1 on the published packaging, 176 of the 225 µs of a token (78%) is dependent latency — a compute chain of 117 µs, 40 µs of collective latency over about 210 on-path collectives, 12 µs of pipeline hops and 7 µs of control — and only 43 µs is the weight sweep (Figure 8-3).

We measured the cost of one dependent boundary on a Blackwell GPU (Figure 4-2). The key finding is that the expensive part is not kernel launch, which programmatic dependent launch and persistent megakernels already remove, but the physics of the dependency itself: a result produced by every streaming multiprocessor must pass through the L2 cache, and a memory fence must wait for L2's acknowledgement. The cheapest all-SM boundary that actually delivers the activation vector costs about a microsecond, and none of the alternative designs we tried — data-carried flags, per-producer flags, cluster hierarchies, or Megatron-style operator pairing — was faster. Only dependencies that stay inside one SM or one cluster are cheap, and a matrix-vector product spread over the whole chip cannot stay there.

A fixed model permits a compiled sequencer to issue dependent operations within a few cycles. The block widths and pipeline depths must then meet the token latency budget (§6.3).

4.4 Challenge 3 — Scaling past one die must not add latency

Frontier models span many dies, so communication, collectives and packaging enter the critical path of every token.

A System-2 model needs tens of reticle dies (Table 4-2). Splitting it adds two kinds of serial cost: pipeline hops, where a token's activations move from the dies holding one group of layers to the next, and collectives, where the dies of a tensor group combine partial results inside a layer. Both are latency-bound, because decode activations are kilobytes, not megabytes. Keeping every die's weights local and moving only activations keeps the bytes small, but the latency does not shrink with them: on shippable packaging a V4.1 token spends a fifth to a quarter of its time on links (Table 4-4), and the collective latency after the last partial sum is the largest single fixed term of the token.

Packaging is a hard constraint rather than a parameter. High-density die-to-die links reach only adjacent dies, board links pay forward-error-correction latency, and interposers are limited in size: four reticle-sized dies with their HBM exceed the 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production in 2026 [TSMC 2026], and the one announced four-die product was reportedly cut back to two [Rubin Ultra]. The packaging choice is therefore evaluated, not assumed (§6.9).

4.5 Challenge 4 — Modern models are irregular, and hardwiring must stay exact and fast

MoE routing, top-k indexers, Sinkhorn-normalised hyper-connections, Engram lookups and sparse attention must become fixed-latency pipelines at about 1 GHz while matching the reference bit for bit — and the reference's own summation order is part of the latency.

The System-2 line of models adds operators that are neither matrix products nor element-wise functions, and each sits on the critical path (Table 4-5). They are data-dependent selections, iterative normalisations and hashed lookups — exactly the operations a conventional datapath implements with loops, sorts and serial reducers. Built that way they cost from a few percent of the per-user rate (a two-pass top-512: 2% at 200K, 9% at 1M) to most of it (an 8-lane stream unit: 1,481 instead of 5,365 tokens/s); each needs its own pipeline, sized to its share of the budget.

The same operators make exactness hard. Floating-point addition is not associative, so a different reduction order is a different model, and faults in selection or indexing produce fluent but wrong tokens. The full-scale compiler lane found three such faults only by comparing against the reference token by token (results/abi3/deepseek_v41_pool_and_decode_path.json). Worse, the reference fixes an order that hardware cannot make fast: the released golden sums sequentially — 2,560 terms in a hyper-connection mix, 2,048 in wo_a, 640 in p·v — and a dependent FP32 chain of n terms takes at least n adder latencies however wide the engine is, 12,800 cycles for the longest at a 5-cycle add. Under those orders the fixed part of a V4.1 token is 841 µs at 200K, which caps it near 1,179 tokens/s at any width (§6.5). Finally, every unit must close timing near 1 GHz on a 7 nm-class process, where a single combinational binary32 addition already takes several nanoseconds.

5From bottlenecks to architecture

5.1 Weight movement dominates decode energy

The first insight concerns energy and bandwidth together. At batch 1 a decode step performs one multiply-accumulate per weight it reads, so the cost of a step is set by how far each weight travels, not by the arithmetic done on it. Architecture work has long observed that moving an operand costs more than operating on it, and recent industry analysis puts data movement at over 80% of the energy of a large AI cluster [He 2026]. Decode is an extreme case of that rule (Challenge 1): its arithmetic intensity is about one operation per byte, so almost all of its energy and time is data movement, and the largest single stream is the weights.

A common-KV reduced-step cross-check combines routed logic activity with modeled HBM traffic (§8.4). HBM weight reads take 276.65 µJ, 82.5% of the HBM-weight step; ROM weight reads take 0.21 µJ, 0.36% of the ROM-weight step. The ROM machine still pays 27.60 µJ for KV HBM reads and 10.17 µJ for the interface idle (Figure 8-1). Two consequences follow. The right place for weights is where they are consumed, which motivates P1; and once weights are local, the next levers are on-chip movement (clock gating, register count) and the per-user state that still streams from HBM, not the arithmetic units.

5.2 Fixing weights and schedule in silicon

The memory and dependency limits of §4.2 and §4.3 share a cause: a general-purpose accelerator must treat both the weights and the schedule as data, fetched and synchronised at run time. For a model that will be served unchanged for months, both can be fixed at fabrication. Weights then become mask ROM placed beside the multipliers, and the schedule becomes a compiled program whose dependencies are resolved before the chip is built.

This removes the memory wall for weights rather than moving it. Each ROM macro delivers a word per cycle to the lanes next to it, so aggregate weight bandwidth grows with silicon area, not with package pins. A ROM bit is also denser than an SRAM bit and far cheaper to read than an HBM access (Table 5-1), which is what lets a System-1 model fit one reticle and a System-2 model fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. With the weights no longer re-read per user, a single user keeps the lanes busy: batching becomes a way to add aggregate throughput, not the price of efficiency.

The insight has costs that bound where it applies. Each model and version needs its own mask set; capacity scales with die count; the KV cache is per-user state and lives in HBM beside the ROM die, so its bandwidth is untouched by ROM; and long-context attention traffic therefore bounds a ROM design exactly as it bounds an HBM one, which is why the workload's state per token matters (§3.2, Table 4-3).

The adopted select uses four quarter memories of 512 lines × 16 scores each (address width 9), with a 76 KB per-die SRAM provision for capacity margin (ca0b82f8, docs/ARCH_SPEC_V41.md §6). How the blocks meet the specification. The weight engines are a pooled block-dot array — FP8 × FP8 and FP4 × FP8 with FP32 accumulation per 32-element block — plus a BF16/FP32 engine for wo_a, the compressor, the router and the index projection; every matrix is striped across enough ROM macros to reach the read rate. The attention engine is separate from them because ROM-stationary lanes cannot take KV operands; V4.1's KV is a single 512-wide latent row shared by all 64 heads, so one row read feeds 16 heads × 512 multiply-accumulates on a die. The indexer keeps its keys in the model's own FP4 codes plus one UE8M0 scale per 32, 68 bytes per key: the golden quantises every key to FP4 before use, so this is lossless and 3.8× smaller than BF16. The select keeps a running lower bound on the 512th-largest score while scores arrive and discards everything below it — exact, because nothing it discards can enter the top 512 — so only about k·(1 + ln(n/k)) scores survive (2,858 of 50,000 per die at 200K, 3,707 of 262,144 at 1M), and a threshold select's two passes run over the survivors; it is built as four 16-lane quarters because a 64-lane threshold select did not globally route. The layer-20 candidate mask is consumed at least four layers later, so its width follows the ingest rate within its ingest-rate budget. The collectives use the one-shot fixed-rank-order engine: each die broadcasts its partial once and folds all N in rank order, so the latency after the last partial is 2 + (N − 1) adder latencies.

Three integration requirements from the dependency chain

At the design widths, the dependency chain sets the token time. Three requirements for the integration core ot_hdc_core_v41x, take it to 134.3 µs (§8.5):

6.5 The arithmetic contract (R-ARITH)

A dependent FP32 chain of n terms cannot finish in fewer than n adder latencies, however wide the engine: 12,800 cycles for the 2,560-term mix at a 5-cycle add. Under the golden orders the fixed part of a 200K token is 841 µs, four times the whole 207 µs target, and the best any width can do is 1,179 tokens/s (1,183 at 8K, 1,156 at 1M). The contract that removes this is the golden's own split_sum with the chunk length fixed instead of the chunk count:

  • every accumulation is cut into contiguous chunks of at most 8 terms, each summed sequentially from +0, and the chunk sums are added by a pairwise tree padded with +0;
  • a tree level that crosses dies is the one-shot collective's fixed rank order;
  • where both operands are FP8/FP4 with 32-element block scales, the chunk is the quantisation block: its dot is formed exactly and rounded once, which is what makes a block-dot lane cost about 78.5 µm² per multiply-accumulate instead of a full FP32 adder's;
  • the golden, the ISA and the RTL change together, and bit-exactness stays defined against the golden.

Status and numerics. The V4.1 golden carries the contract as a mode (HDC_V41_ARITH=chunk8, selectable per operator class so re-specified units can be brought up one class at a time), and the ISA-level simulator is bit-exact with it in every logit at position 7 and over a prompt plus four generated tokens. Against the sequential reference order the logits differ by at most 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ up to position 7; at position 8 a routed-expert selection flips at a near-tie of the reduced vehicle's 12-expert router, and later differences follow such flips. Neither order is closer to the oracle (the vendor model in BF16 on a GPU): the sequential reference matches 3 of 16 teacher-forced steps and chunk-8 matches 2, with mean oracle-token gaps of 0.345 and 0.341, and both reproduce the oracle's first token with the same margin (results/arch/v41_arith_contract.json). The Qwen3 vector stream unit's reducer implements the same contract. A second revision (R-ARITH v2: the normalisation's reciprocal root folded into the consuming matrix products' outputs, online softmax in 64-entry blocks) is committed as a further golden mode and is required by the first dependency-chain lever.

Exactness costs nothing once the order is chosen for hardware; a sequential order written for a reference implementation costs 78% of the token.

On both ROM designs each user's KV cache — and on V4.1 its compressed rows and index keys — lives in HBM stacks attached to the ROM die, never in on-die SRAM; SRAM holds buffers and staging only. In on-die SRAM, an 8K cache left the Qwen3 reticle no room for its own 4-bit weights (§4.2), and the V4.1 array had carried an 8,192-token admission limit; in HBM, capacity is no longer the limit: six 24 GB stacks hold 201 users at 8K with FP8 KV on the Qwen3 reticle, and four stacks per V4.1 layer die hold 5,018 users at 200K and 962 at 1M (93.5 MB per user on the busiest die). Prefetch. The Qwen3 cache for layer l + 1 (positions below the current token, which do not depend on it) streams while layer l computes, into a 33.6 MB two-layer buffer; the token's own K/V row stays on die and is written back behind the stream. On V4.1 the window rows and the reuse layers' selections have static addresses and are prefetched a layer ahead; an index-source layer's gather waits for its top-512 and is budgeted at 250 ns to its first row. Index keys stay in the model's own FP4 codes with one UE8M0 scale per 32 elements — 68 bytes per key, lossless because the golden quantises every key to FP4 before use, and 3.8× smaller than BF16.

The controller is a design problem, not a given. A stream at 90% of raw peak must survive DRAM refresh. The HBM comparator's refresh study measured three things on the RTL (reduced vehicles, every run bit-exact; Table 6-10). All-bank refresh alone costs about 9% of raw peak, so it just meets a 90% floor on the Qwen3 stream. A controller with a shallow queue lets one refreshing pseudo-channel stall an in-order stream for about the refresh time: with 64-beat queues, all-bank refresh fell to 0.852 at 8 pseudo-channels and cost up to 15% of a V4.1 token on the comparator vehicle. And the best refresh policy depends on the access pattern: a refresh-aware per-bank scheduler that refreshes the not-yet-refreshed bank the fewest queued bursts need reaches 0.965–0.993, but on the indexer's long sequential key scan its tie-break must favour the most recently activated bank (0.956 of peak per stack) rather than the closed-bank-first choice that suits weight streams (0.851).

6.8 Lane copies, area and power

A lane copy is a multiply-accumulate lane that shares its group's weight word, so m copies let one weight read serve m positions — speculative slots or batched users — but never speed a single position. Routed at 528 µm² per lane, two copies cost the Qwen3 reticle 138 mm², paid for by the SRAM the KV cache vacated. Power, not area, then bounds the reticle: at the signed-off MAC energy the die exceeds its cooling limit.

Lane copies. ot_hdc_lane_copy is 16 lanes of the exact BF16 multiplier and the circulating FP32 adder with its interleave registers, sharing the group's weight word, split tree, result port and argmax. It routed on ASAP7 at 8,449 µm², closed at a 0.9 ns target with 67 ps of setup slack (about 1.2 GHz): 528.08 µm² per lane, against 1,071.8 µm² for a whole matrix-engine lane (results/physical_abi3/asap7/hdc/ot_hdc_lane_copy/physical.json). One copy of all 131,072 lanes is therefore 69.2 mm². 6.9 Multi-die fabric and packaging (C3)

Multi-die systems keep every die's weights local and move only activations (P4). A tensor group of four dies spans a pair of shippable two-die packages; its fixed-order collectives return bit-identical results on every die; between tensor groups, a pipeline of layer groups passes one token's state. Link latency after the last partial sum, not bandwidth, is what remains on the critical path.

Qwen3-8B single reticle

The adopted design specifies 8,192 lane groups with two lane copies, six HBM PHYs on the edge, a 33.6 MB KV prefetch buffer and the drafter's ROM; none of it is yet a die, and the core has not been simulated as a whole at shipped shape. DeepSeek-V4.1 ROM array

Forty layers, each an attention half and a MoE half joined by hyper-connections (a four-copy residual stream). Layers share state in groups: 2–7 read layer 2's compressed KV and index selection, 8–13 read 8's, 14–19 read 14's, and 20–39 read layer 20's compressed KV and candidate-block mask. Layers 1 and 14 add Engram lookups. A package boundary never separates a KV source from a consumer it cannot reach cheaply.

Physical organisation. The array is 188 reticle dies of 815 mm² in 94 two-die packages — the shipping, B200-class package on an interposer of about 3.3 reticles. A tensor group of four dies spans a pair of neighbouring packages: every matrix, including a quarter of every expert, is split four ways; each all-reduce is reduced inside each package over UCIe, exchanged once over the package link and broadcast inside the package. The 40 layers are packed onto the dies in order in 28 layer groups, so a token visits 28 pipeline stages, and the ring's closing link carries the next embedding row from the head group directly to stage 0. The four head dies each store a quarter of both the output head and token embedding, so the argmax winner reads the next embedding row locally. Each layer die streams its KV and index keys from four HBM3E stacks — eight per two-die package. The Engram-table dies have no HBM; the four head dies carry four stacks each for the draft KV (results/arch/v41_lanes.json, commit b07a5745). The committed baseline adds two shippable levers to plain option (b): a light-FEC package link on the on-module channel between the two packages of a tensor group (a 112G PAM4 channel with a reduced-interleave FEC, 130 ns per hop, range 100–170, at unchanged bandwidth, instead of full RS(544,514) at 209 ns; the rack-cable stage hops keep full RS(544,514), because a light FEC is not qualified on CR-class copper, §8.9) and overlapped reductions (a collective's bytes stream behind its producer, so only the latency after the last partial stays on the path). Plain (b) is kept as the named alternative in every table.

The proposed compressed-KV HBM map stripes groups of sixteen rows across the four tensor dies and their four stacks, with separate main and index regions and per-layer window rings. The executable contract is in runtime/prefill/v41_hbm_placement.py. Placement profile v2 (commit 81c18201, docs/V41_HBM_KV_PLACEMENT.md) uses the ingest engine’s lossless 68-byte index-key super-blocks rather than 96-byte padded linear rows. At 1M context its all-layer, owner-only envelope reserves 754,974,720 bytes for main rows, 178,257,920 for index keys and 11,141,120 for window rings: 944,373,760 bytes across 16 die/stack pairs. In the 28-stage placement the busiest owner stage reserves 93,462,528 bytes per die per user; its busiest stack reserves 655,212,544 bytes at the 28-user fill. 6.11 Host interface and runtime

A host interface makes the design usable as a server: descriptor rings, DMA and an OpenAI-compatible endpoint drive the RTL and return tokens bit-exactly.

An AXI4-Lite register block and a 64-bit AXI4 DMA master present the chip like a PCIe endpoint. Requests arrive on a submission ring of 32-byte descriptors (user slot, prompt, max new tokens, EOS ids; greedy only); each generated token returns as a 16-byte completion entry, so the completion ring is the token stream. Sixteen user slots match the package controller. A runtime drives the Verilated chip through shared host memory and serves /v1/models, /v1/completions and /v1/chat/completions with streaming. The endpoint returns 1073, 382, 93 bit-exactly from the RTL; the interface routes at 1,135 MHz and passes sign-off (docs/HOST_INTERFACE_AND_RUNTIME.md).

7.4 Hierarchical full-chip implementation

A hierarchical flow characterises each block's ports, derives timing budgets from the tile's paths, the floorplan distances and a wire model fitted to routed links (0.60 ps/µm + 190 ps), hardens blocks against their budgets with LEF/Liberty abstracts, and assembles tiles and reduced 2×2 dies with post-route timing at every block pin. Mesh ports use a pipelined credit link so every tile pin is a register. Its lessons are now physical rules for every new block: every block boundary is registered, inputs and outputs (a registered 512-bit output needs about 300 ps inside the block, with the router's clock-to-pin about 270 ps); clock insertion on the matrix engine takes about 540 ps; repair needs a 40% slew margin and a 15 ps setup margin for estimated against extracted wire delay; and routes carry a real hold margin.

Power, IR drop, clock and corners

We find that clock and register power dominate every block, that a block-level power grid must be backed by an upper grid to hold IR drop, and that the design has setup margin at the slow corner only with a relaxed clock and fails hold at the fast corner. Power is computed from the switching activity of the real decode campaigns: RTL register activity is mapped onto the routed flops and propagated through the logic by OpenSTA. Checked against a full gate-level simulation of one collective, this method matches the register and clock power within 1.4% but overstates combinational power 2.1×, so every total is an upper bound on its combinational part (docs/POWER_CLOCK_SIGNOFF.md). Where the factor comes from. ROM ÷ GPU splits into an architectural factor and efficiency inside the HBM class. The architectural factor is ROM ÷ the HBM-class ceiling, 5.5–5.8× (5.54× in the record at the budget target, 5.8× at the calibrated rate): what storing weights beside the lanes buys over streaming them at the stacks' full bandwidth. The rest is the HBM class not reaching its own ceiling: HDC-HBM runs at 0.90 of it, and the calibrated H200 at FP8 at 37%, because a GPU pays a fixed per-token cost at batch 1. That second factor is partly software, and our own measurement shows how much: vLLM decodes on the RTX PRO 6000 at 80% of that GPU's BF16 bandwidth roofline and 65% at FP8, while the H200 fit implies 37–44%. An H200 at the locally measured fractions would decode 384 tokens/s at FP8, and the apparent ROM/GPU gap would narrow substantially. The robust comparison is therefore against the HBM class — the ceiling and the same core on HBM — where the ROM leads by 5.5–6.5×; the GPU rows carry up to about 1.8× of software efficiency that a better-tuned stack recovers. At 2K the KV stream no longer binds: the calibrated core takes 108,393 cycles per token (about 10,100 tokens/s) and the budget target is 10,465, set by the weight sweep.

8.6 Speculative decoding: a gift for HBM, earned on ROM with lane copies

Speculative decoding is a cure for the memory wall. On an HBM machine a verify pass rides on one read of the weights and the speed-up approaches the acceptance length: 3.6× on the Qwen3-8B comparator. On a ROM machine a verify pass costs its positions in multiply-accumulates, so speculation pays only when lane copies let one weight read serve several positions: with none it gains nothing; with three, DFlash gives the Qwen3-8B reticle 1.77× and carries it past its KV floor, because the verify slots share one read of the cache; with two, MTP gives the V4.1 array 2.2×. Routed experts break the rule on both machines: six positions touch 34.6 distinct experts, not six.

How speculation works. A drafter proposes a block of B − 1 tokens; the target scores all B positions in one pass and keeps the longest prefix that matches its own greedy choice, plus one token of its own. Under greedy decoding the output is identical to plain decoding, token for token; our golden, ISA and RTL keep it bit-exact because every verify position uses the same fixed-order arithmetic as a one-position step (§6.5). The speed-up is the accepted length per pass, τ, divided by the cost of a pass relative to a plain step. For Qwen3-8B we use DFlash [DFlash], a 1.05 B-parameter block-diffusion drafter that fills a whole block in one parallel pass; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ships its own drafter, DSpark (three stages over a block of five) [DSpark].

Acceptance is a property of the workload

We measured τ on the real Qwen3-8B with the released DFlash drafter on eleven workloads (Table 8-15). The spread is the finding: thinking-mode reasoning, the System-2 use of §2, accepts 3.4–3.9 tokens per pass, less than half the 8.2 we measure on the same MATH-500 prompts with thinking off — which reproduces the paper's 8.01 — and agentic workloads range from 2.6 (web navigation) to 11.3 (JSON). We therefore price every speculative figure at the pooled τ = 4.1 (561 blocks on the real model in an FP32 reference) and treat the paper's 6.5–8.0 only as a sensitivity. For V4.1 no acceptance has been measured on the real model; τ = 4.1 is a decision, bracketed by 3.27–3.80 (derived from a published survival curve) and about 5 (reported for SGLang with DSpark) [vLLM DSpark, LMSYS DSpark].

The two machines respond differently

Speculation trades arithmetic for memory traffic: a verify pass does B positions of multiply-accumulates for one read of each weight. An HBM machine decoding one user is bandwidth-bound, so its multipliers idle while weights stream; the extra positions ride on the same read and the pass costs little more than a plain step. A hardwired ROM machine has already removed that bottleneck: each lane consumes one weight per cycle, so a B-position pass costs about B times the lane time and the gain collapses to about τ / B. Lane copies change this: with m copies sharing each weight word (§6.8), a pass costs ⌈B/m⌉ sweeps, and the best block is about m. And on the Qwen3-8B reticle a second effect appears: the verify slots also share one read of each user's KV cache, so speculation divides the KV stream per token by the tokens per pass — the only lever that takes the reticle past its KV floor.

8.7 Batching: per-user rate, throughput and energy

Batching does not make a ROM design faster per user, and it fills the two designs differently. The Qwen3-8B reticle is KV-bound from the second user: its total throughput is flat at 8,941 tokens/s from batch 2, a ceiling the same core on HBM approaches only near 128 users. The V4.1 array is a 28-stage pipeline: each user keeps the batch-1 rate up to 28 users, and the adopted design reaches 891 K tokens/s at 200K and 689 K at 1M for 1,024 users, 13.2× and 10.8× the best HBM comparator (results/arch/v41_hbm_switched.json, commit b07a5745), while energy per token falls as the stages fill.

Where the energy goes. In the Qwen3-8B production model, 61.4 of the 82.4 mJ per token is allocated to DRAM stacks, 59.5 mJ of it the 604 MB of FP8 KV; the physical die-versus-stack split awaits characterization because the transferred A100 HBM value includes controller energy. the die's 21.0 mJ is leakage (5.9), clock (4.3), the HBM interface (3.9), the KV ring's SRAM traffic (3.3), multiply-accumulates (1.7), the ROM read and delivery (1.0) and the stream unit (1.0). The KV format is therefore the largest energy lever as well as the largest speed lever, and the ratio to the HBM core moves with the 13.1 pJ/bit stack figure, which carries most of the ROM's energy. The same core on HBM at the same weights spends 541 mJ, 143 mJ of it on its die, mostly clock and leakage over a token that takes 0.73 ms. On the V4.1 array the chips' static power, 14.9 kW, is 98% of a batch-1 token; filling the pipeline divides it among 28 users. The hardwired schedule bounds the power. Its saturated worst case has every lane copy multiply-accumulate every cycle, the stream unit and the ROM read path busy every cycle and the stacks at full raw bandwidth; no program can exceed it, so it is provisioned at 1.2× rather than at nameplate. A GPU tensor core's reported 1.4 pJ per MAC shows that target is not exotic.

8.10 Design-space exploration: every rejected alternative as an ablation

Every alternative we evaluated either lowered the per-user rate or kept it at the cost of a harder build; none was rejected on taste. In the specification model the largest single effects are the reduction order, the stream-unit width and the engines' widths; the wafer is rejected on aggregate throughput, HBM attach and manufacturing, not on batch-1 latency.

Wafer-scale integration

What it was. The V4.1 package logic laid out as reticle fields stitched on one wafer, so a die-to-die hop becomes an on-wafer field crossing, with KV in HBM stacks around the wafer edge, compared with the array at equal silicon (docs/WAFER_VS_ARRAY_ISO_AREA.md; both priced by the analytical critical-path sensitivity model, the array on shipping two-die packages).

What we found. On a published stitched mesh (125 ns per 28.55 mm field crossing) the wafer is slower at batch 1, 0.81–0.94 of the array, because collectives across its many fields dominate. With a designed express fabric the batch-1 comparison becomes a near tie: the wafer matches the array whenever a crossing costs about 56–71 ns or less, our routed express link crosses a field in about 30 ns, and at 25 ns the wafer leads by 3–11% with the built divider (up to 15% at the wire limit). The wafer loses on aggregate throughput — from three wafers up the array delivers 1.33–1.65× the wafer's aggregate at 4,096 users under every fabric, because at equal silicon it carries 6.5× the HBM stacks — on HBM attach, since a wafer's perimeter gives 43 stacks against 5 per 815 mm² die and must also carry the wafer-to-wafer SerDes, and on manufacturing: in-place defect tolerance instead of discarding bad dies, cross-field stitching, an express long-wire fabric measured only as one link, and whole-wafer test. The manufacturing comparison is qualitative — no yield or cost model exists for either machine — but every item except the array's HBM count per package is harder for the wafer.

9Related work

GPU decode. Serving stacks capture decode in CUDA graphs and fuse element-wise work into neighbouring kernels; Programmatic Dependent Launch overlaps a kernel's prologue with its predecessor. Megakernels run a forward pass as one persistent kernel whose instructions wait on counters in memory: Hazy Research's Llama-1B megakernel [Hazy] and the Mirage Persistent Kernel [MPK]. They remove launch overhead but keep the weight stream (C1) and the L2 round trips of cross-SM dependencies (C2), which we measure directly. Vendor-measured concurrency-1 rates [NIM, GPUStack] calibrate our GPU rows; our own measurement shows that batch-1 efficiency against the roofline varies by nearly 2× across stacks (§8.4).

Communication overlap. Fused kernels overlap tensor-parallel communication with computation on GPUs, hiding up to 96% of it for GEMMs [FLUX] and speeding communication-heavy sublayers by 30% on average [T3]; TensorRT-LLM fuses the all-reduce with the following RMSNorm for DeepSeek on B200 [TRT-LLM], and in-fabric reductions cut the latency of small collectives [NVLink SHARP]. We adopt the overlap for the bytes, and find that in decode the latency after the last partial sum remains on the critical path.

Speculative decoding. Draft-and-verify decoding is lossless under greedy and rejection sampling [Leviathan]. Drafters range from feature-level heads (EAGLE-3 [EAGLE-3]) and built-in multi-token prediction (DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-V3], DSpark on DeepSeek-V4 [DSpark]) to block-diffusion drafters that fill a block in one pass (DFlash [DFlash]); serving engines report DSpark's acceptance only as bounds or verify-window lengths [vLLM DSpark, LMSYS DSpark]. All of them were designed for bandwidth-bound GPUs; we show that their benefit depends on the machine's binding resource, that on a ROM machine it returns only with lane copies that share a weight read, and that the acceptance on thinking-mode workloads is half the published figure (§8.6).

On-chip-SRAM dataflow machines. Groq's tensor streaming processor makes execution deterministic by removing reactive hardware (arbiters, caches) and scheduling everything in the compiler [TSP], a principle we share (P2, P5). Cerebras keeps weights in on-wafer SRAM (vendor-reported 44 GB on 46,225 mm² for WSE-3 [WSE-3]). Both trade capacity for bandwidth with SRAM; ROM's density advantage is what lets a large MoE fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. SambaNova (tiered SRAM/HBM/DDR reconfigurable dataflow), Tenstorrent (mesh of programmable cores) and d-Matrix (SRAM digital in-memory compute) occupy other points of the same trade-off [citation needed].

Model-specific silicon. Etched builds a transformer-specific datapath that keeps weights in HBM [citation needed]. Taalas HC1 hardwires Llama-3.1-8B on one 815 mm² TSMC 6 nm die and reports about 17,000 tokens/s per user [HC1], the closest prior work and our calibration anchor. We differ in targeting a large sparse System-2 model across dies at 200K and 1M context, in keeping per-user state in attached HBM, in designing top-down from a per-token budget with a published arithmetic contract and bit-exact verification chain, and in comparing against the same core on HBM at equal logic area.

Packaging. Two-die packages on interposers of about 3.3 reticles are in volume production; TSMC reports 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production and larger classes to come [TSMC 2026, CoWoS], and the one announced four-die GPU was reportedly cut back to two dies [Rubin Ultra]. Board-reach 112G PAM4 links pay 100 ns or more for KP4 FEC [UCIe PHY, IEEE 802.3df]; the OIF CEI-112G agreements and UALink allow lighter FEC [OIF CEI-112G, UALink], which is the basis of our on-module link; on rack copper IEEE 802.3ck mandates RS(544,514) [802.3ck]. Broadcom's Scale-Up Ethernet budgets under 100 ns for an endpoint's link and PHY and under 250 ns for a switch [SUE], and GB200 all-reduce kernels near their speed-of-light floor take 1.4–2.4 µs [SoL]. HPC interconnects. Special-purpose machines have long bought latency with fixed function: Anton 3 reaches a neighbour node in about 55 ns at 34 ns per hop [Anton 3], Blue Gene/Q's torus about 35 ns per hop [BG/Q], and HPE Slingshot 350 ns per switch [Slingshot]. Our cross-package figures sit in the same range, which is why the synchronisation advantage between packages is modest (§8.9).

10Discussion

10.1 Operating energy and fixed cost

The modeled operating energy is lower at the batch sizes where per-user speed matters. A full cost-per-token result still needs die cost, yield, packaging and amortized design cost, which are not quantified here. The source-pinned reduced-step cross-check gives 5.7× lower energy for ROM weights with the same HBM KV path on both sides; routed logic activity and modeled memory bytes supply that ratio (§8.4). That comparison holds the logic fixed, so it isolates the memory technology; its absolute values are ASAP7-predictive. The specification models extend it to the design points under assumed production energy inputs (Table 10-1). At batch 1 the Qwen3-8B reticle spends 82.4 mJ per token against 541 mJ for the same core on HBM at the same 3.5-bit weights (6.6×); most of the ROM's energy is its own KV stream, so the ratio is sensitive to the 13.1 pJ/bit HBM assumption. The adopted V4.1 design at 1M, with static power and switches charged to both machines at the wall, uses 3.8× less energy per token than the best HBM comparator at batch 1 (2.34 against 8.83 J; 4.2× with MTP), where both are static-dominated, and 10.2× less at the 28-user pipeline fill (128 against 1,301 mJ; 15.5× with MTP); at 200K the same points are 4.0×, 4.4×, 12.4× and 17.7× (results/arch/v41_hbm_switched.json, commit b07a5745). The comparator's switch trays are an assumed 1.5 kW each; its modelled wall power per package, 0.60–0.98 kW, sits within the range of reported B200 power figures. The fill figure is the energy headline. The advantage narrows with batch on Qwen3-8B, because per-user KV traffic, not weights, dominates both machines' energy (83.1 against 89.2 mJ at 128 users, §8.7), and it holds on V4.1, where the array's energy per token falls with batch as well. Power provisioning favours the fixed schedule too: its saturated worst case bounds its draw, so the V4.1 rack is provisioned at 58.3 kW against 132 kW for a GB200 NVL72 (§8.8).

Throughput per silicon is not the price of speed either. The Qwen3-8B reticle's total throughput from two users on (8,941 tokens/s, KV-bound) is above what the same core on HBM reaches at 128 users (8,573), and the adopted V4.1 design's aggregate is 7.1× the best HBM array's at the 28-user fill at 1M (11.6× with MTP). The energy comparisons use the production-value power inputs in §8.8.

The fixed cost includes the mask set and design. Leading-edge mask sets are estimated in the tens of millions of dollars and complete designs at 7 nm at $50–75 M [SemiAnalysis]. Because the ROM is via-programmed, a new model or version changes the via layers and the ROM contents, not the logic (docs/MEMORY_COMPILERS_AND_BIST.md), so the per-model cost is a partial mask set [citation needed for its price] on an otherwise reused design. The fixed cost is amortised over the tokens served in the model's deployment [citation needed for typical volumes], and latency has value of its own for the workloads of Table 4-1, which a cost-per-token metric does not capture.

10.2 Why build silicon for one model?

Three observations make a model-specific chip reasonable. First, the paired System-1/System-2 structure separates a slowly changing interaction layer from a replaceable background model; OpenAI describes the background model of GPT-Live as swappable without replacing the real-time layer [GPT-Live], so the model that most needs speed is also the one that changes least. Second, frontier open architectures are converging: latent or compressed KV, learned sparse attention and mixtures of experts recur across successive generations [DS-V2, DS-V3.2, DS-V4], so the units a model like V4.1 needs are likely to be needed again. Third, only the ROM contents are model-specific: the vector stream unit and arithmetic contract are shared between the Qwen3 and V4.1 cores, and the unit library, the top-down budget tools, the verification chain and the physical flow carry over.

The costs are real. A model cannot be updated in the field; a new version needs new via masks and a new die run; capacity is fixed at fabrication; and a model whose value lies in frequent retraining is a poor fit. Deployment lifetimes of production models are not well documented publicly [citation needed], and the economics of §10.1 depend on them.

10.3 Best achievable today, and what comes after

The adopted V4.1 array uses validated package and rack links, in-switch reduction and four HBM3E stacks per die. The next gains depend on measured integrated behavior: closing the 1.087 GHz whole-core route, confirming collective overlap, and reducing the remaining package collective latency. If silicon misses the latency target, the area envelope allows wider attention, index and weight engines, but their full-core benefit must be remeasured. For Qwen3-8B at 8K, the FP8 KV stream is the rate limit and lane energy is the power limit; changes to either require a new accuracy or physical result.

10.4 Limitations

The headline rates are models, not silicon. The Qwen3-8B rate is the RTL-calibrated timing model — fitted within a few cycles to the RTL on the reduced vehicle — run at the specification's widths on the real program, and it assumes a design-shape FP8 KV path whose scalar HBM and vector write components pass separate RTL gates, while integrated vector reads and writes remain open; the V4.1 rates are the specification model, verified on the program by an event replay, with independent attention and vector benches, the combined reduced pooled-indexer and QE weight-HBM gate, and the whole-core route still pending. The clocks are the slowest routed units, not closed cores. The per-user rates are calibrated only against one self-reported product; memory macros are model-grade views on a predictive PDK; the GPU boundary costs are measured on a workstation Blackwell (GB202), not a B200; and V4.1's acceptance length is a decision, not a measurement. ROM removes weight traffic, not per-user state traffic, so models whose state grows with context (Table 3-1) gain less, and at long context the ROM ÷ HBM advantage shrinks (Table 8-11).

11Conclusion

Fast per-user decode is limited on today's accelerators by weight bandwidth, by the synchronisation of a long dependency chain, and at long context by the bandwidth of each user's KV cache. Fixing the model in silicon removes the first and turns the second into a design problem: mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of area, and a compiled schedule of fixed-latency units turns on-chip synchronisation into a few cycles, while between packages it leaves only fixed-function link crossings — but only if every unit is built to a per-token budget. Designing top-down from that budget, with an arithmetic contract chosen for hardware and per-user state in attached HBM, the calibrated Qwen3-8B model projects 8,910 tokens/s at 8K, within 0.3% of its ideal KV bandwidth ceiling, and the DeepSeek-V4.1 one-rack design projects 8,622 tokens/s per user at 1M (23,578 with native speculation) — conditional on collective overlap and the 1.087 GHz system clock, neither yet demonstrated on the whole system — 2.4× an HBM array of equal logic area in its best configuration on shipping hardware (2.0× when both speculate), with 10–15× less energy per token at a filled pipeline, and a rack provisioned at 58.3 kW, under half an NVL72, all on link and power values checked against their standards. What remains to settle is the whole-core simulation and routing of the adopted cores, the FP8 KV streamer, a multiply-accumulate lane within the power budget, and the accuracy of the quantised formats; each is marked pending with the record that will settle it.
