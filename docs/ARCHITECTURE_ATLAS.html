OpenTallas: Hardwiring Language Models for Order-of-Magnitude Faster Tokens

Mask-ROM weights beside the multipliers, each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and a decode dataflow whose every block is sized from a per-token budget aim at two design points. One Qwen3-8B user at 8K context on a two-reticle package, every layer split across both dies (tensor-parallel 2), decoding with DFlash speculation, gets an 18,720-token/s design rate; without speculation it decodes 10,968 tokens/s. The package is liquid-cooled, a GB200-class two-die package that allows 474.6 W a die, so the 457 W and 438 W each die draws on the proposed production lane run uncapped; with the lane as built the rates cap at 6,680 and 7,859 tokens/s. One DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash user at 1M context gets 7,049 tokens/s (7,286 at 200K) from a one-rack array of shippable two-die packages. Without speculation and uncapped, the two designs are 12.5× and 2.0× model-specific HBM comparators under matched logic-area budgets, each comparator at its best modeled configuration. These are model rates (for Qwen3-8B the specification chain with DFlash acceptance measured in BF16, beside an RTL-calibrated autoregressive chain; for V4.1 a design-point model with bench-measured collective tails and modelled on-die wire, not measured chip throughput) on link and power values checked against published standards and datasheets; reduced versions of both models decode bit-exactly in RTL, and the adopted blocks are passing their per-block benches. The Qwen3-8B weights are 8-bit (signed INT8 with a BF16 scale per output row), and in the deployment arithmetic they meet our quality threshold (WikiText-2 perplexity −1.59% at 2K, −1.03% at 8K; MMLU −0.5 points); its single-token RTL gates reach 2,048 positions, so 8K is not yet proven in RTL.

1Abstract

Interactive and agentic use of language models is gated by per-user decode speed, which batching does not improve. We find that decode is dominated by data movement, and that the costliest data to move are the weights. Once the weights stop moving, a token becomes a latency problem — the critical path of one layer times the number of layers — plus, at long context, a KV-bandwidth problem. We present OpenTallas, a model-specific architecture that stores weights in mask ROM beside the multiply-accumulate lanes, keeps each user's KV cache in attached HBM, and executes decode as a compiled dataflow of fixed-latency units whose widths and depths are derived top-down from a per-token budget. We build it for a small interactive model (Qwen3-8B on a two-reticle package, 8K context) and a large sparse long-context model (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash on 188 reticle dies in shippable two-die packages, 1M context primary and 200K secondary). Qwen3-8B decodes with DFlash speculation, which the design requires. Its 8-bit weights do not fit one reticle, so every layer is split across two reticle dies in one package (tensor-parallel 2), joined by a UCIe link, with each user's KV in eight attached HBM3E stacks. At batch 1, four lane copies beside every lane give it a design rate of 18,720 tokens/s per user (specification model; draft, verify and commit in series at block 5; acceptance measured in BF16 on a GPU). At that rate each die draws 457 W on the proposed production lane, within the 474.6 W per die of the liquid-cooled two-die package the design uses, so it runs uncapped; with the lane as built it is capped at 6,680 tokens/s (power scenarios B and A), and air cooling, 374.6 W a die, would cap even the production lane at 14,176. Without speculation, the RTL-calibrated chain targets 10,968 tokens/s at specification widths, 92% of the ideal FP8 KV bandwidth ceiling of 11,921 tokens/s and 3% short of its 11,325 target: its 100,169-cycle dependency chain — one die's slice of every layer, the INT8 scale multiply after each matrix and 1,419 cycles of die-to-die exchanges — is longer than the 92,161-cycle KV stream floor. Liquid cooling leaves it uncapped on the production lane (438 W a die) and caps it at 7,859 with the lane as built; air would cap the production lane at 8,921. At its uncapped design rate it is 12.5× the same package streaming its 8-bit weights from its eight HBM stacks. The 8-bit weight format meets the pre-set quality threshold in the deployment arithmetic with FP8 KV: WikiText-2 perplexity changes by −1.59% at 2K and −1.03% at 8K and MMLU by −0.5 points against the vendor BF16 model; lower-bit formats down to 3.5 bits did not. Its single-token RTL gates reach 2,048 positions, so 8K is unproven. For V4.1 at 1M, the adopted design decodes 7,049 tokens/s (15,890 with its native MTP at an acceptance of 3.65; 7,286 and 16,449 at 200K), a design-point model with bench-measured collective tails and modelled on-die wire, not measured chip throughput: an RTL stage bench found that the collectives do not fully overlap their producers (rack gate C7 not met), and the adopted collective levers, each bit-exact in that bench, recover about 58% of the loss (full overlap would give 7,374); every long wire of the layer die is registered at its per-cycle reach, and the 21 µs of on-die wire those traversals expose on the 143 µs token is charged (without it the design would reach 8,246). That is 2.0× an HBM array of equal logic area in that array's best configuration on shipping hardware — a 96-die tensor group in one NVL72-class switched domain — and 1.8× with MTP on both, conditional on the 1.087 GHz system clock, not yet demonstrated on the whole system; on one power model for both machines (logic dies and their HBM stacks, static power and each user's KV reads from HBM charged to both), its energy per token is 6.8–9.3× lower at a filled 28-user pipeline and 2.8× lower at batch 1. The adopted scan rebalance keeps the hottest die within the 474.6 W liquid limit at every modeled point through 1M, with 383–471 W across the 1M production-lane operating points; its helper exchange and engage policy still need an RTL bench. The compiled schedule's synchronisation advantage is an on-chip property — a dependent handoff costs 5 cycles in RTL against 371 ns measured on a GB202 GPU — while across packages both machines pay link physics and the modeled advantage narrows to 8–13× the best measured GB200 collective. Keeping bit-exactness at these rates required a new arithmetic contract: the reference's sequential sums alone cap V4.1 near 1,170 tokens/s at any width. These are model rates, not silicon: the RTL decodes reduced versions of both models bit-exactly and the adopted blocks meet their per-block benches, but whole-core token simulation and routing are pending. Under the power scenarios the Qwen3-8B package with DFlash spends 55.4 mJ per token at batch 1 on the proposed production lane (126 mJ on our measured lane; 96 and 130 mJ without speculation), most of it streaming its KV cache from HBM; a common-HBM-KV reduced-step cross-check gives a derived 5.7× energy ratio for ROM over HBM weight storage.

2Introduction

A growing class of language-model workloads is limited by how fast one user's tokens arrive, not by how many tokens a data centre can produce. Interactive System-1 models sit in a conversation or a control loop. Human turn-taking leaves gaps of about 200 ms across languages [Stivers], and full-duplex speech models are built to answer within that budget (Moshi reports 200 ms in practice [Moshi]); voice agents such as GPT-Live [GPT-Live] and Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models [TML] hold the conversation on a fast model for exactly this reason. In-car and home assistants inherit the same conversational budget [citation needed], and in robotics and driving the budget is set by physics: fast-slow systems run a large reasoning model at about 5 Hz beside a 20 Hz control loop because the large model cannot keep up with the vehicle [Fast-slow VLA].

Background System-2 models reason before they act, so their wall-clock time is the length of the reasoning trace times the time per output token. Computer-use agents today take tens of minutes on OSWorld tasks that take a person a few minutes, and most of that time is large-model calls [OSWorld-Human]. Background speed also becomes foreground latency: generative user interfaces are designed and coded per request, and Google reports its implementation "can sometimes take a minute or more" [Generative UI]; self-improving software agents iterate write–test–evaluate loops whose duration is the sum of their generated tokens [AlphaEvolve].

Serving systems and hardware roadmaps, however, optimise cost per token. Continuous batching and paged KV caches [vLLM] maximise tokens per second per accelerator, and batching amortises weight reads across users without making any one stream faster. Public trackers such as Artificial Analysis report per-user output speed by model and over time [Artificial Analysis]; whether the output speed of frontier models is improving or declining is not something we could verify from that source [citation needed]. What is clear from first principles (§4) and from our own measurement is that one GPU decodes an 8B model for one user at a few hundred tokens per second: 94 tokens/s at BF16 and 151 at FP8 on a Blackwell workstation GPU we measured, and 217 on an H200 at FP8, calibrated to NVIDIA's own measurements.

Two deployment questions follow. What is the energy cost? With a common HBM KV path, the reduced Qwen3 step uses 5.7× less derived energy with ROM weights than with HBM weights (ASAP7 routed logic plus modeled memory bytes). On the power scenarios' sourced inputs, the Qwen3-8B package spends 96 mJ per token at batch 1 on the production lane (scenario B) against 1,284 mJ for the same package on HBM at the same 8-bit weights (13.3×; on our routed lane, scenario A, 130 against 1,318 mJ, 10.1×); most of the ROM package's energy is the KV stream's HBM path, which both machines pay. The V4.1 array at 1M spends 2.40 J per token at batch 1 — logic dies and their HBM stacks, with static power, the always-on links and each user's KV reads from HBM charged to both machines on one power model — against 6.70 J for the best HBM comparator, and 151 mJ against 1,023 mJ at a filled 28-user pipeline, and falls further as the pipeline fills (§8.8); its modeled aggregate throughput at equal silicon rises with that fill (§8.7); and because the schedule is fixed, its saturated worst case bounds its power, so the whole rack is provisioned at 39.9 kW against 132 kW for a GB200 NVL72. Mask-set amortization depends on deployment volume and remains unquantified (§10.1). Why build silicon for one model? Because the interactive layer of a paired system changes slowly, frontier architectures are converging, and everything except the ROM contents is reused across models (§10.2).

Our thesis is that when a model is fixed, both its weights and its schedule can be hardware. Mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of silicon area, and a schedule compiled before fabrication replaces run-time synchronisation with sequencing measured in cycles. What is left is latency: the critical path of each layer, times the number of layers, plus the time to stream each user's KV cache. The design problem therefore becomes a budgeting problem — derive from a per-token target how wide and how shallow every block must be, keep every operator exact, and check each block against its budget before the whole core is built. The approach has real costs, a mask set per model and capacity that scales with die count, which we treat as first-class constraints (§5, §10).

This paper makes the following contributions:

  • The source-pinned energy cross-check that decode energy is data movement, not arithmetic, and that moving weights is its largest part: on a common-KV reduced step, HBM weights cost 5.7× the derived energy of ROM weights (§5.1, §8.4).
  • A characterisation of fast per-user decode as four challenges — the memory wall for weights and, at long context, KV; the dependency chain that remains once weights are free; multi-die latency; and irregular exact operators — each tied to a design principle (§4, §5).
  • A top-down design method — target, binding resource, budget, per-block requirements, microarchitecture, per-block performance benches — with executable budgets for both workloads, and source-pinned evaluation of the adopted designs with their evidence limits (§6.3, §8.5).
  • An arithmetic contract (chunks of eight, summed by a pairwise tree) that keeps every operator bit-exact against a golden model while removing the sequential sums that alone would cap V4.1 near 1,170 tokens/s (§6.5).
  • Decode cores for both models — a 1,024-lane vector stream unit, K-split attention, dedicated weight, attention and indexer engines, a streaming exact top-512 select, a per-bank Engram gather — with KV in attached HBM behind a refresh-aware controller, and lane copies measured at 528 µm² per lane that make speculation pay on ROM (§6.4–§6.8).
  • An evaluation at the design points: iso-area Qwen3-8B against a two-die GPU and the same package on HBM; V4.1 at 200K and 1M on shippable packaging; speculative decoding with acceptance measured on eleven workloads; batching from 1 to 1,024 users with energy; synchronisation cost against a GB200 NVL72; and the power each design draws and must be provisioned for, on link and power values checked against normative sources and datasheets (§8).
  • Implementation evidence on ASAP7: routed units, memory compilers with self-test and repair, scan and ATPG, a hierarchical flow that assembles tiles and reduced dies (full-chip integration pending) and an OpenAI-compatible host interface (§7).

3Background

3.1 Anatomy of one decode token

A decoder-only model produces one token per step. Each of its L layers applies, in order: a normalisation; projections of the hidden vector into queries, keys and values (matrix-vector products against the layer's weights); attention of the new query over every stored key and value of this user's context (the KV cache), appending the new key and value; an output projection; a second normalisation; and a feed-forward block (dense, or a mixture of experts of which a router picks a few). A final normalisation and a vocabulary projection produce logits, and the chosen token becomes the next step's input.

  • Every step depends on the one before. A matrix-vector product needs the whole previous vector; softmax needs every score; the next token needs every logit. A token is a chain of on the order of ten dependent steps per layer.
  • At batch 1 each weight is used once per token. Two FLOPs per weight read, so the work is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound.
  • Per-user state grows with context. The KV cache is read every step; its size per token decides whether long context is a bandwidth problem of its own.

3.2 Representative workloads

Deployed systems increasingly pair two classes of model. A small, fast "System-1" model holds the interaction: it must answer within human conversational latency. A large "System-2" model does deeper work in the background and is delegated to when needed. OpenAI's GPT-Live (July 2026) keeps continuous voice interaction on a low-latency model and delegates search, reasoning and agentic work to GPT-5.5 running in the background [GPT-Live]. Thinking Machines Lab's interaction models (May 2026) state the same split: "when a task requires deeper reasoning than can be produced instantaneously, the interaction model delegates to a background model that runs asynchronously" [TML]. Both classes are latency-bound: the first for the user's turn, the second because agentic loops chain many calls.

We therefore pick one representative of each class rather than the models most favourable to ROM:

  • System-1: Qwen3-8B [Qwen3], a widely used dense GQA model of the size class used for fast interaction. It is also small enough to fit one package of two reticle dies, the smallest multi-die case (Challenge 3).
  • System-2: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, a large sparse mixture of experts (552 B total, 16 B active) with compressed KV for a 1 M-token context. It carries the architectural line frontier open models are following for long context: latent/compressed KV (MLA, DeepSeek-V2 [DS-V2]), sparse attention selected by a learned indexer (DeepSeek-V3.2 [DS-V3.2]), compressed sparse attention to one million tokens (DeepSeek-V4 [DS-V4]), plus manifold-constrained hyper-connections with a Sinkhorn projection [mHC] and Engram conditional-memory lookup [Engram]. If future frontier models follow this line, their decode will look like V4.1's: small active weights, fixed attention work per token, context-dependent cost confined to an indexer, and many irregular operators (Challenge 4).

Selection criteria, applied to every profiled candidate. (1) Class: is it a representative System-1 or System-2 model? (2) Openness: released weights and configuration, so a bit-exact golden can be written. (3) Per-user state: does per-token state traffic stay small at long context, or does it dominate regardless of where weights live? (4) Architectural novelty relative to the other target. (5) ROM capacity at the analytical model's density.

4Motivation

4.1 Why speed, not cost

The workloads of §2 each carry a latency budget set by a person or by physics, and in every case the budget is spent mostly on decode. Table 4-1 collects them. The common thread is that a faster token cannot be bought with more accelerators: batching and data parallelism raise aggregate throughput, while one stream's rate is bounded by the per-token critical path. Cost per token is therefore the wrong objective for these workloads; time per output token for one user is the right one, subject to a cost per token that is not worse (§10.1).

4.2 Challenge 1 — The memory wall sets the per-user ceiling: for weights always, for KV at long context

Every token touches every active weight and each user's whole KV cache, so where they live — their bandwidth and their capacity together — bounds single-user speed.

At batch 1 each weight is used for exactly one multiply-accumulate per token, so decode performs about one floating-point operation per byte of weight read. An accelerator that keeps the weights in HBM therefore runs at the speed of its memory interface, not its arithmetic: the per-user rate cannot exceed memory bandwidth divided by the bytes a token must read. Batching raises aggregate throughput by reusing each read across users, but it leaves the rate of any one user where it was.

The ceiling is low for both workload classes. For the System-1 model it lies in the hundreds of tokens per second even on the fastest current HBM part, and a sparse System-2 model does not escape it: its active weights per token are comparable to a dense 8B model's (Figure 4-1). Moving the weights next to the arithmetic changes the picture qualitatively, because on-die memory bandwidth grows with the number of memory macros rather than with the number of package pins.

The KV cache is the second wall, and it does not move into ROM. A Qwen3-8B token at 8K context reads each user's whole cache once: 1.21 GB at BF16, or 604 MB at FP8 (Table 4-3). Streamed from eight HBM3E stacks at 7.2 TB/s sustained, the FP8 cache alone takes 83.9 µs — 92,161 cycles, more than twice the 38,493-cycle sweep of the weights over 196,608 lanes — so even a machine whose weights cost nothing to read cannot exceed about 11,900 tokens/s per user at 8K, and a BF16 cache halves that. DeepSeek-V4.1 compresses its attention state to a fixed 13.9 MB per token at any context, but its lightning indexer scores every compressed key: 38.5 MB of index keys and 2.3 G multiply-accumulates per token at 200K, and 182.7 MB and 11.0 G at 1M, where the indexer's arithmetic exceeds that of the six routed experts (8.5 G). In V4.1, context enters the token only through the indexer (docs/ARCH_SPEC_V41.md §2).

The difficulty on the weight side is capacity. On-die storage must hold the whole model, and the densities differ by an order of magnitude between memory technologies (Table 4-2). We find that even the System-1 model does not fit one reticle at 8 bits a weight: its INT8 weights take 449 mm² of the design's compute-in-ROM cells and its drafter 58 mm² more, 64 mm² beyond what one 815 mm² reticle has left beside its lanes, stream unit and HBM PHYs (results/arch/qwen3_budget.json area). A one-die product would need about 4-bit weights, as the only published hardwired product uses [HC1]; for Qwen3-8B a lower-bit format failed our quality threshold (docs/ARCH_SPEC_QWEN3.md, evidence note), so the design spans two reticle dies in one package. SRAM-only storage would need about a dozen wafers for the System-2 model. Per-user state competes for the same area, so on both ROM designs each user's cache lives in HBM beside the dies (§6.7).

4.3 Challenge 2 — Once weights are free, a token is a dependency chain

When weights no longer bound the rate, a token's time is the critical path of one layer times the number of layers, and on a GPU each dependent boundary costs on the order of a microsecond.

A decode layer is a chain: each matrix-vector product needs the whole output of the previous step, softmax needs every score, and the next token needs every logit. We count 20 dependent stages in a Qwen3-8B layer, so a token is 720 stages long; a V4.1 layer is about twice as deep and adds collectives between dies. Every cycle a stage exposes is paid 720 times on Qwen3-8B. With the weights swept at the rate the lanes allow (38,493 cycles), the Qwen3-8B target leaves 29,103 cycles for exposed latency: at most 40 cycles per dependent stage, fewer than one fast exponential pipeline takes (49). For V4.1 on the published packaging, 176 of the 225 µs of a token (78%) is dependent latency — a compute chain of 117 µs, 40 µs of collective latency over about 210 on-path collectives, 12 µs of pipeline hops and 7 µs of control — and only 43 µs is the weight sweep (Figure 8-3).

We measured the cost of one dependent boundary on a Blackwell GPU (Figure 4-2). The key finding is that the expensive part is not kernel launch, which programmatic dependent launch and persistent megakernels already remove, but the physics of the dependency itself: a result produced by every streaming multiprocessor must pass through the L2 cache, and a memory fence must wait for L2's acknowledgement. The cheapest all-SM boundary that actually delivers the activation vector costs about a microsecond, and none of the alternative designs we tried — data-carried flags, per-producer flags, cluster hierarchies, or Megatron-style operator pairing — was faster. Only dependencies that stay inside one SM or one cluster are cheap, and a matrix-vector product spread over the whole chip cannot stay there.

A fixed model permits a compiled sequencer to issue dependent operations within a few cycles. The block widths and pipeline depths must then meet the token latency budget (§6.3).

4.4 Challenge 3 — Scaling past one die must not add latency

Frontier models span many dies, so communication, collectives and packaging enter the critical path of every token.

A System-2 model needs tens of reticle dies (Table 4-2), and even the System-1 model at 8-bit weights needs two; splitting every Qwen3-8B layer across its two dies puts 73 short exchanges on each token's chain (1,419 cycles with the embedding-row handoff, 1.4% of it; §6.4.1). Splitting a model adds two kinds of serial cost: pipeline hops, where a token's activations move from the dies holding one group of layers to the next, and collectives, where the dies of a tensor group combine partial results inside a layer. Both are latency-bound, because decode activations are kilobytes, not megabytes. Keeping every die's weights local and moving only activations keeps the bytes small, but the latency does not shrink with them: on shippable packaging a V4.1 token spends about a quarter of its time on links (Table 4-4), and the collective latency after the last partial sum is the largest single fixed term of the token.

Packaging is a hard constraint rather than a parameter. High-density die-to-die links reach only adjacent dies, board links pay forward-error-correction latency, and interposers are limited in size: four reticle-sized dies with their HBM exceed the 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production in 2026 [TSMC 2026], and the one announced four-die product was reportedly cut back to two [Rubin Ultra]. The packaging choice is therefore evaluated, not assumed (§6.9).

4.5 Challenge 4 — Modern models are irregular, and hardwiring must stay exact and fast

MoE routing, top-k indexers, Sinkhorn-normalised hyper-connections, Engram lookups and sparse attention must become fixed-latency pipelines at about 1 GHz while matching the reference bit for bit — and the reference's own summation order is part of the latency.

The System-2 line of models adds operators that are neither matrix products nor element-wise functions, and each sits on the critical path (Table 4-5). They are data-dependent selections, iterative normalisations and hashed lookups — exactly the operations a conventional datapath implements with loops, sorts and serial reducers. Built that way they cost from a few percent of the per-user rate (a two-pass top-512: 2% at 200K, 9% at 1M) to most of it (an 8-lane stream unit: 1,487 instead of 5,418 tokens/s); each needs its own pipeline, sized to its share of the budget.

The same operators make exactness hard. Floating-point addition is not associative, so a different reduction order is a different model, and faults in selection or indexing produce fluent but wrong tokens. The full-scale compiler lane found three such faults only by comparing against the reference token by token (results/abi3/deepseek_v41_pool_and_decode_path.json). Worse, the reference fixes an order that hardware cannot make fast: the released golden sums sequentially — 2,560 terms in a hyper-connection mix, 2,048 in wo_a, 640 in p·v — and a dependent FP32 chain of n terms takes at least n adder latencies however wide the engine is, 12,800 cycles for the longest at a 5-cycle add. Under those orders the fixed part of a V4.1 token is 844 µs at 200K, which caps it near 1,171 tokens/s at any width (§6.5). Finally, every unit must close timing near 1 GHz on a 7 nm-class process, where a single combinational binary32 addition already takes several nanoseconds.

5From bottlenecks to architecture

5.1 Weight movement dominates decode energy

The first insight concerns energy and bandwidth together. At batch 1 a decode step performs one multiply-accumulate per weight it reads, so the cost of a step is set by how far each weight travels, not by the arithmetic done on it. Architecture work has long observed that moving an operand costs more than operating on it, and recent industry analysis puts data movement at over 80% of the energy of a large AI cluster [He 2026]. Decode is an extreme case of that rule (Challenge 1): its arithmetic intensity is about one operation per byte, so almost all of its energy and time is data movement, and the largest single stream is the weights.

A common-KV reduced-step cross-check combines routed logic activity with modeled HBM traffic (§8.4). HBM weight reads take 276.65 µJ, 82.5% of the HBM-weight step; ROM weight reads take 0.21 µJ, 0.36% of the ROM-weight step. The ROM machine still pays 27.60 µJ for KV HBM reads and 10.17 µJ for the interface idle (Figure 8-1). Two consequences follow. The right place for weights is where they are consumed, which motivates the first principle, stationary weights (§5.3); and once weights are local, the next levers are on-chip movement (clock gating, register count) and the per-user state that still streams from HBM, not the arithmetic units.

5.2 Fixing weights and schedule in silicon

The memory and dependency limits of §4.2 and §4.3 share a cause: a general-purpose accelerator must treat both the weights and the schedule as data, fetched and synchronised at run time. For a model that will be served unchanged for months, both can be fixed at fabrication. Weights then become mask ROM placed beside the multipliers, and the schedule becomes a compiled program whose dependencies are resolved before the chip is built.

This removes the memory wall for weights rather than moving it. Each ROM macro delivers a word per cycle to the lanes next to it, so aggregate weight bandwidth grows with silicon area, not with package pins. A ROM bit is also denser than an SRAM bit and far cheaper to read than an HBM access (Table 5-1), which is what lets a System-1 model fit one two-reticle package and a System-2 model fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. With the weights no longer re-read per user, a single user keeps the lanes busy: batching becomes a way to add aggregate throughput, not the price of efficiency.

The insight has costs that bound where it applies. Each model and version needs its own mask set; capacity scales with die count; the KV cache is per-user state and lives in HBM beside the ROM die, so its bandwidth is untouched by ROM; and long-context attention traffic therefore bounds a ROM design exactly as it bounds an HBM one, which is why the workload's state per token matters (§3.2, Table 4-3).

6.4.1 Qwen3-8B two-reticle package

The adopted Qwen3-8B design spans two reticle dies in one package with every layer split across both: each die holds half of every layer's query and KV heads, FFN columns and vocabulary rows in INT8 ROM beside 6,144 lane groups (98,304 lanes, four lane copies each), one 1,024-element-per-cycle vector stream unit and four HBM3E stacks for its own KV heads; K-split attention, prefetched one-cycle issue, three-cycle floating-point units and a 904-instruction token program (one die's slice) are as before. The split across the two dies. Every layer runs on both dies (tensor-parallel 2; results/arch/qwen3_budget.json die_split): die 0 holds query heads 0–15 with KV heads 0–3, FFN columns 0–6,143 and vocabulary rows 0–75,967, die 1 the other halves. The column-parallel products (QKV, gate/up, the LM head) need no exchange; the row-parallel O and down projections end in partial sums that are all-reduced over the package's UCIe link, and the two vocabulary halves exchange their argmax candidates once a token: 2 × 36 + 1 = 73 exchanges, each a tagged transaction carrying a 16,384-byte FP32 vector each way (1.18 MB a token with the embedding row), repeated identically every token (docs/QWEN_TWO_RETICLE_RTL_CONTRACT.md). Every exchange sits on the dependency chain, because the residual add and RMSNorm that follow need the whole reduced vector. At the configured 10 ns hop (configs/hardware/technology.json links.rom_package_ucie), 4.3 cycles of transfer at 4.2 TB/s and a 4-cycle FP32 add, an exchange costs 19.3 cycles: with the 12-cycle embedding-row handoff, 1,419 cycles a token, 1.4% of the chain (850–3,045 cycles over the link's 3–30 ns hop range). Its energy, 9.5 µJ a token at 0.5 pJ/bit, is negligible. The partials cross in FP32 because the golden folds the dies' FP32 matrix outputs in rank order; BF16 partials would change the golden. Each die's specification chain also carries the INT8 scale multiply after every matrix and the golden's K-splits (the drafter's fc slice at S = 4,096, the P.V split at a power of two). A contiguous layer cut instead (layers 0–19 on die A, 20–35 with the head on die B) crosses the link only twice a token, but each layer then runs on one die's groups and streams its KV from that die's four stacks, so the KV floor doubles and binds: 5,960 tokens/s autoregressive and 12,106 with DFlash, against 10,968 and 18,720 (results/arch/qwen3_budget.json layer_cut_alternative). TP-2 is adopted.

The ROM-read bound. Each die's lanes consume one 8-bit weight a lane a cycle, 98,304 bytes a cycle at 6,144 groups. The die's half of the swept ROM (207.6 mm² of the layers' and the LM head's weights; the embedding is a lookup) supplies 1.017 times that at the 8-bit read density and the 0.75 sustained read efficiency (results/arch/qwen3_budget.json area.rom_read); the next step of 512 groups would have 0.939 of what it needs. So 6,144 groups a die are the most the ROM feeds, and the lane copies, which share their group's weight word, add multiply-accumulates without adding reads.

In the calibrated replay of one die's slice at its 6,144 groups, the matrix sweep takes 38,880 cycles (0.990 of the package's ideal), K-split attention 9,216 cycles a pass, the vector unit 10,809 busy cycles, with one-cycle prefetched issue. The dependency requirement of at most 40 exposed cycles per stage is still missed, and at 8K the chain, not the KV stream, sets the rate. A dependent FP32 chain of n terms cannot finish in fewer than n adder latencies, however wide the engine: 12,800 cycles for the 2,560-term mix at a 5-cycle add. Under the golden orders the fixed part of a 200K token is 844 µs, four times the whole 207 µs target, and the best any width can do is 1,171 tokens/s (1,175 at 8K, 1,147 at 1M). The contract that removes this is the golden's own split_sum with the chunk length fixed instead of the chunk count:

  • every accumulation is cut into contiguous chunks of at most 8 terms, each summed sequentially from +0, and the chunk sums are added by a pairwise tree padded with +0;
  • a tree level that crosses dies is the one-shot collective's fixed rank order;
  • where both operands are FP8/FP4 with 32-element block scales, the chunk is the quantisation block: its dot is formed exactly and rounded once, which is what makes a block-dot lane cost about 78.5 µm² per multiply-accumulate instead of a full FP32 adder's;
  • the golden, the ISA and the RTL change together, and bit-exactness stays defined against the golden.

Status and numerics. The V4.1 golden carries the contract as a mode (HDC_V41_ARITH=chunk8, selectable per operator class so re-specified units can be brought up one class at a time), and the ISA-level simulator is bit-exact with it in every logit at position 7 and over a prompt plus four generated tokens. Against the sequential reference order the logits differ by at most 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ up to position 7; at position 8 a routed-expert selection flips at a near-tie of the reduced vehicle's 12-expert router, and later differences follow such flips. Neither order is closer to the oracle (the vendor model in BF16 on a GPU): the sequential reference matches 3 of 16 teacher-forced steps and chunk-8 matches 2, with mean oracle-token gaps of 0.345 and 0.341, and both reproduce the oracle's first token with the same margin (results/arch/v41_arith_contract.json). The Qwen3 vector stream unit's reducer implements the same contract. On the full Qwen3-8B the contract is quality-neutral: an exact GPU emulation of the Qwen3 golden (bit-exact with it on the reduced vehicle) at 8,192 lane groups — the package's 12,288 groups split some dot products differently and are not separately evaluated — changes WikiText-2 perplexity by −0.14% at 2K and −0.09% at 8K against the vendor BF16 arithmetic and MMLU by −0.3 points, and its top-1 disagreements fall on BF16 logit ties (results/quality/qwen3_8b_deployment_arithmetic.json). Running the full model needs the reciprocal's saturation: for finite d above 1.6 × 10³⁸ the bit seed would wrap, so the golden and RTL seed +0 and return +0 — an explicit saturation contract (+0), not IEEE rounding; the true reciprocal is subnormal — which SiLU reaches for every gate at or below −87.98. A second revision, online softmax in 64-entry blocks, is committed as a further golden mode and is required by the first dependency-chain lever; folding the normalisation's reciprocal root into the consuming matrix products is rejected because it changes the checkpoint's FP8 quantisation point.

Exactness costs nothing once the order is chosen for hardware; a sequential order written for a reference implementation costs 78% of the token.

6.6 Special units for irregular operators

Each irregular operator of Challenge 4 becomes its own fixed-latency pipeline, specified by the V4.1 golden and verified bit-exact. The two that most affect latency are the Sinkhorn unit and the threshold select.

The adopted ot_hdc_core_v41x dispatches to dedicated weight, attention, pooled-index, vector, selection, Engram and hyper-connection engines. The quantised block-dot and activation-quantisation primitives serve its QE path. Source-pinned reduced-model campaigns have produced bit-exact complete tokens with every adopted unit enabled; the timed HBM gates exercise QE weights and pooled index keys. The current switched five-package all-unit run is still in progress. The die-top smoke also runs one reduced token bit-exactly with attention KV fetched through the die's HBM prefetch path.

Sinkhorn and selection. The mHC path projects a 4×4 matrix through repeated Sinkhorn normalisation [mHC]. The adopted vector/SFU path and streaming selection engine implement the chosen arithmetic and position-order tie rules. Their reduced-model exactness is checked with the same golden and ISA contract as the rest of the V41x core; isolated physical routes constrain individual pieces, not the combined die clock.

The cost is an HBM term in every ROM budget (Table 4-3) and the stacks' PHYs on the dies' edges: four 10 mm² PHYs on each Qwen3 die, whose free edge — the perimeter less the edge it shares with the other die — holds four stacks of 12 mm at the 60% edge-use rule, as each of a B200's two dies carries four.

Prefetch. The Qwen3 cache for layer l + 1 (positions below the current token, which do not depend on it) streams while layer l computes, into a ring on each die that holds one layer plus the package's refresh cover (19.3 MB; the die's own KV heads need half); the token's own K/V row stays on die and is written back behind the stream. On V4.1 the window rows and the reuse layers' selections have static addresses and are prefetched a layer ahead; an index-source layer's gather waits for its top-512 and is budgeted at 250 ns to its first row. Index keys stay in the model's own FP4 codes with one UE8M0 scale per 32 elements — 68 bytes per key, lossless because the golden quantises every key to FP4 before use, and 3.8× smaller than BF16.

The controller is a design problem, not a given. A stream at 90% of raw peak must survive DRAM refresh. The HBM comparator's refresh study measured three things on the RTL (reduced vehicles, every run bit-exact; Table 6-10). All-bank refresh alone costs about 9% of raw peak, so it just meets a 90% floor on the Qwen3 stream. A controller with a shallow queue lets one refreshing pseudo-channel stall an in-order stream for about the refresh time: with 64-beat queues, all-bank refresh fell to 0.852 at 8 pseudo-channels and cost up to 15% of a V4.1 token on the comparator vehicle. And the best refresh policy depends on the access pattern: a refresh-aware per-bank scheduler that refreshes the not-yet-refreshed bank the fewest queued bursts need reaches 0.965–0.993, but on the indexer's long sequential key scan its tie-break must favour the most recently activated bank (0.956 of peak per stack) rather than the closed-bank-first choice that suits weight streams (0.851).

Prefill and KV ingest

Every prefill runs on GPUs; the decode chips ingest the KV. Prefill processes a prompt's positions together and is bound by arithmetic, which a GPU sustains well; decode is the dependency chain the ROM designs are built for. Neither ROM design therefore prefills on chip: a cold prompt and every agent turn are prefilled on a GPU tier, which sends the resulting KV into the per-user HBM layout above (results/arch/prefill_ingest.json, tools/arch_prefill.py; transfer contracts in runtime/prefill/). One DGX B200 (eight B200s) prefills a cold 1M-token V4.1 prompt in 4.8 s at the conservative calibrated rate and 3.4 s at the optimistic one; the lightning indexer's scan, quadratic over a cold prompt, is 25% of its 4.8 × 1016 FLOPs, and at half the calibrated rate on the indexer alone the prefill takes 5.9 s. A 200K prompt takes 0.73 s. The GPUs send 890 bytes per context token — each owner layer's 356-byte compressed row and index key at 2.5 owner rows per token, 0.94 GB at 1M — and the rack switch multicasts every row to each stage that reads it, so 7.84 GB is written into the array's HBM. The Qwen3-8B package takes 142 ms of prefill for an 8K prompt on one H200.

Late binding keeps the user slot free. The GPU holds a prompt's KV until its prefill ends and then bursts it to the array: 9.6 ms at 1M over two 400G uplinks, bound by the NICs, against 3.5 ms for the busiest package's switch port and 3.1 ms for the RTL-measured ingest engine on the busiest die. The decode slot is reserved only for the burst — 0.009% of a 32,768-token session at 1M, against 4.4% if the KV streamed into a reserved slot during the prefill — so the time to first token is the prefill plus about 10 ms (§8.4, Table 8-T1). On the Qwen3 package the KV arrives through a PCIe endpoint on die 0 behind the host's 400G NIC (die 1's KV heads cross the UCIe link) and streams layer by layer during the prefill, leaving 0.7 ms exposed.

Evidence classes. The GPU prefill rates are calibrated from third-party measurements, not measured here: DeepSeek-V3 prefill on GB200 NVL72 [LMSYS GB200] with BF16 attention sets the conservative rate, 28% of a B200's dense FP8 peak (39% for the FP8/NVFP4 configuration, the optimistic rate), and NVIDIA's NIM time to first token for an 8B model at 20K sets the Qwen3 rate [NIM]. The ingest engine's rates are measured in RTL (results/rtl/hdc_kv_ingest_campaign.json). The NIC goodput is a published GPUDirect RDMA measurement on ConnectX-7, 391 Gb/s [CX-7 GDR]. No prefill-to-first-token path has been measured end to end.

6.8 Lane copies, area and power

A lane copy is a multiply-accumulate lane that shares its group's weight word, so m copies let one weight read serve m positions — speculative slots or batched users — but never speed a single position. Routed at 528 µm² per lane, four copies cost each die of the Qwen3 package 208 mm², fitted in what the die has left beside its half of the 8-bit ROM. Power, not area, then bounds the package: at the signed-off MAC energy each die exceeds its cooling limit.

Lane copies. ot_hdc_lane_copy is 16 lanes of the exact BF16 multiplier and the circulating FP32 adder with its interleave registers, sharing the group's weight word, split tree, result port and argmax. It routed on ASAP7 at 8,449 µm², closed at a 0.9 ns target with 67 ps of setup slack (about 1.2 GHz): 528.08 µm² per lane, against 1,071.8 µm² for a whole matrix-engine lane (results/physical_abi3/asap7/hdc/ot_hdc_lane_copy/physical.json). One copy of a Qwen3 die's 98,304 lanes is therefore 51.9 mm². On the V4.1 die the MTP design point uses m = 2, which doubles every engine and the specification's block area from 99.5 to 199 mm²; m = 3 buys 12% more for 50% more area, and m = 4 would exceed the 329 mm² envelope.

6.9 Multi-die fabric and packaging

Multi-die systems keep every die's weights local and move only activations, with deterministic collectives (§5.3). A tensor group of four dies spans a pair of shippable two-die packages; its fixed-order collectives return bit-identical results on every die; between tensor groups, a pipeline of layer groups passes one token's state. Link latency after the last partial sum, not bandwidth, is what remains on the critical path.

Qwen3-8B two-reticle package

Pending · whole-die implementation of the Qwen3 package

The adopted design specifies, on each of two dies, 6,144 lane groups with four lane copies, INT8 ROM with per-channel scales, four HBM PHYs on the free edge, a UCIe PHY on the shared edge, a 19.3 MB KV ring and half the drafter's ROM; none of it is yet a die, the credit-controlled UCIe exchange and the 8-bit weight path have no RTL, and the core has not been simulated as a whole at shipped shape. Settled by: the whole-core token simulation and place-and-route of the adopted core, after the FP8 KV streamer lands (§6.4.1), through the hierarchical flow's Qwen tile and 2×2 reduced die (tools/chip_assembly/, §7.4).

DeepSeek-V4.1 ROM array

Forty layers, each an attention half and a MoE half joined by hyper-connections (a four-copy residual stream). Layers share state in groups: 2–7 read layer 2's compressed KV and index selection, 8–13 read 8's, 14–19 read 14's, and 20–39 read layer 20's compressed KV and candidate-block mask. Layers 1 and 14 add Engram lookups. A package boundary never separates a KV source from a consumer it cannot reach cheaply.

Physical organisation. The array is 188 reticle dies of 815 mm² in 94 two-die packages — the shipping, B200-class package on an interposer of about 3.3 reticles. A tensor group of four dies spans a pair of neighbouring packages: every matrix, including a quarter of every expert, is split four ways; each all-reduce is reduced inside each package over UCIe, exchanged once over the package link and broadcast inside the package. The 40 layers are packed onto the dies in order in 28 layer groups, so a token visits 28 pipeline stages, and the ring's closing link carries the next embedding row from the head group directly to stage 0. The four head dies each store a quarter of both the output head and token embedding, so the argmax winner reads the next embedding row locally. Each layer die streams its KV and index keys from four HBM3E stacks — eight per two-die package. The Engram-table dies have no HBM; the four head dies carry four stacks each for the draft KV (results/arch/v41_lanes.json). The committed baseline adds two shippable levers to plain option (b): a light-FEC package link on the on-module channel between the two packages of a tensor group (a 112G PAM4 channel with a reduced-interleave FEC, 130 ns per hop, range 100–170, at unchanged bandwidth, instead of full RS(544,514) at 209 ns; the rack-cable stage hops keep full RS(544,514), because a light FEC is not qualified on CR-class copper, §8.9) and overlapped reductions (a collective's bytes stream behind its producer, so only the latency after the last partial stays on the path). Plain (b) appears only as the published graph the requirement starts from.

KV ingest path

The GPU prefill pod reaches the array through the rack switch that already serves the Engram tables and the host (Figure 6-6): two 400G uplinks, expandable to eight. A prefilled row leaves GPU HBM as a GPUDirect RDMA write over RoCEv2, with no bounce through host memory; the switch multicasts it to the package port of every stage that reads it, and that package's ingest engine places it in HBM at the address the decode core will read (rtl/hdc/ingest/ot_hdc_kv_ingest.sv; placement contract runtime/prefill/v41_hbm_placement.py). At two uplinks a 1M burst is bound by the NICs and occupies the busiest package's single 400G-class switch port at 37% of its rate; at eight uplinks the burst falls to 3.5 ms, bound by that port, which it then fills. That port also carries the Engram gathers of layers 1 and 14, which are on the token path: prefetched from the token id, they must land within layer 1's slack. The switch therefore needs a strict-priority traffic class for Engram above KV ingest, so that a burst never queues a gather behind it (results/arch/prefill_ingest.json switch_port_load). This path is a link-rate model; neither the multicast nor the traffic classes have been simulated.

6.11 Host interface and runtime

A descriptor-ring host interface, DMA and an OpenAI-compatible endpoint have been exercised with reduced RTL vehicles. Binding them to the adopted Qwen HBM-KV tile and V41x die remains a system integration gate.

The host interface exposes AXI4-Lite control and a 64-bit AXI4 DMA master. Requests use 32-byte descriptors, and each generated token returns as a 16-byte completion entry. The source-pinned reduced host campaign demonstrates token delivery through the endpoint and routes the interface block at 1,135 MHz (docs/HOST_INTERFACE_AND_RUNTIME.md). Its tested cores and memory organizations are narrower than the proposed chips, so the campaign is interface evidence rather than a completed full-system token run.

The production descriptor carries ingested KV, not a prompt. Because every prefill runs on GPUs (§6.7), a production submission names a user slot whose KV has already been ingested: it carries the fence tag of the last ingest descriptor, and decoding starts only once the ingest engine has signalled that tag, which the engine's descriptors already provide (rtl/hdc/ingest/ot_hdc_kv_ingest.sv). The interface also needs an egress path. When a GPU's prefix cache no longer holds a user's context, an agent turn must first read that user's KV back out of the array's HBM by RDMA read: 0.93 GB before a 4,096-token turn on a 1M context, 9.5 ms serially over two 400G uplinks. Pipelined by layer, so that the GPU starts on the first layer group while the rest is in flight, the readback lifts the turn's time to first token only from 23.2 ms to 25.6 ms (Table 8-T1). Neither the fence-tagged submission nor the egress path is in the host-interface RTL.

7.4 Hierarchical full-chip implementation

A hierarchical flow characterises each block's ports, derives timing budgets from the tile's paths, the floorplan distances and a wire model fitted to routed links (0.60 ps/µm + 190 ps), hardens blocks against their budgets with LEF/Liberty abstracts, and assembles tiles and reduced 2×2 dies with post-route timing at every block pin. The Qwen tile and reduced die use this flow. The adopted V4.1x core has an RTL tile and layer die (ot_chip_v41x_tile, ot_chip_v41x_die): a reduced token runs through the die top bit-exactly with attention KV prefetched from four behavioural HBM3E stacks shared with the pooled indexer; the die has no floorplan or route yet. Mesh ports use a pipelined credit link so every tile pin is a register. Its lessons are now physical rules for every new block: every block boundary is registered, inputs and outputs (a registered 512-bit output needs about 300 ps inside the block, with the router's clock-to-pin about 270 ps); clock insertion on the matrix engine takes about 540 ps; repair needs a 40% slew margin and a 15 ps setup margin for estimated against extracted wire delay; and routes carry a real hold margin.

8.4 Per-user rate at the design points

At batch 1 the Qwen3-8B package operates with DFlash at an 18,720-token/s design rate per user at 8K in the specification model. Liquid-cooled, it runs uncapped on the production lane and is capped at 6,680 on the lane as built (§8.6, §8.8). Without speculation, the calibrated model targets 10,968 tokens/s (7,859 on the lane as built). At uncapped autoregressive design rates, that is 12.5× the same package on eight HBM stacks, and 7.1× with DFlash on both. No record prices a capped ROM ÷ HBM ratio, so no capped ratio is quoted. The GPU comparisons in the attribution ladder separate software, format and architecture effects. Where the factor comes from. ROM ÷ GPU splits into an architectural factor and efficiency inside the HBM class. On this silicon the HBM class reaches its roof: the same package streaming its 8-bit weights runs at the 0.90 sustained fraction of its eight stacks (881 tokens/s), where the B200's idealised roof at the same weight width also sits, so the architectural factor — what storing weights beside the lanes buys over streaming them at the stacks' sustained bandwidth — is the whole 12.5× (24.1× against BF16 weights). The rest is a GPU not reaching its own roof: the cited B200 application point is half its BF16 roof, because a GPU pays a fixed per-token cost at batch 1. That factor is partly software, and our own measurement shows how much: vLLM decodes 94 tokens/s at BF16 and 151 at FP8 on the RTX PRO 6000, while the NIM-calibrated H200 FP8 row is 217 tokens/s at 8K; these workloads, runtimes and contexts differ. The robust comparison is therefore against the HBM class — the same package on HBM and the B200 roof at the same weight width — where the ROM leads by 12.5×; the GPU rows carry software efficiency that a better-tuned stack recovers. At 2K the KV stream no longer binds the budget: the calibrated core takes 84,469 cycles per token (13,006 tokens/s) and the budget target is 15,698, set by the weight sweep.

The overlap the conditional row assumes was tested in RTL: a stage bench drives the one-shot all-reduce engine from the design point's own producer schedules, over credit queues and links at the validated latencies and rates (results/rtl/v41_stage_collective_campaign.json, all 44 cases bit-exact). Four levers, each measured bit-exact in the same bench on the lever engine ot_rom_oneshot_die_px (results/rtl/v41_collective_levers_campaign.json), shorten the tails. A receive-side relay, in which each package link carries half the words and the receiving die forwards them to its package peer over UCIe, combined with 4-word gathers cuts the KV-rows all-gather from 653 to 309 cycles; the relay with 3-cycle fold adders cuts the all-reduces from 254 to 195; a partial split of the stage hop, 20 of its 81 words over one package's cables and across the package link, cuts it from 473 to 419; and hc_post starts on the arriving all-reduce words instead of the last. C7 with a real core in the loop. The stage and lever benches drive the engines from stub producers, so they leave open whether a real core's production and consumption change the tails. A reduced stage now tests that for the all-reduce class (results/rtl/v41x_real_core_collective_stage.json). The adopted V4.1x core produces rank 0's partials, four one-shot engines fold them over credit links, and the same core consumes each committed word; every rank, word and lane carries a distinct value, and 2-word producer queues and receive FIFOs are held saturated. The stage is exact at cross-package delays of 142 and 228 cycles, the 130 ns light-FEC hop and the 209 ns cable, with no mismatch, fault or read before commit. We reran the unchanged stub bench at the same size (8 words of 16 lanes, both depths 2; results/arch/v41_c7_realcore_vs_stub.json). Ranks 0 and 1, on the core's package, receive every reduced word on exactly the stub's cycle, and the wave period is identical at both delays (2 × delay + 2 cycles per two words). Ranks 2 and 3 receive each word a constant 29 cycles later, which is the core's time to its first partial, not tail growth. What the stub does not measure is the consumer: the core finishes 54 cycles after the last reduced word, and 77 after rank 0's, because this bench invokes it once per 16-lane word at 39 cycles each. The design prices hc_post as a streaming consumer that starts on the first arriving word, which the bench does not exercise. 8.6 Speculative decoding: a gift for HBM, earned on ROM with lane copies

Speculative decoding is a cure for the memory wall. On an HBM machine a verify pass rides on one read of the weights and the speed-up approaches the acceptance length: 3.0× on the Qwen3-8B comparator with its 8-bit weights at block 16, where τ is 3.66. On a ROM machine a verify pass costs its positions in multiply-accumulates, so speculation pays only when lane copies let one weight read serve several positions: with none it gains nothing; with four (m = 5), DFlash gives the Qwen3-8B package 2.08× over its specification chain and carries it past its KV floor, because the verify slots share one read of the cache; with two, MTP gives the adopted V4.1 array 2.9×. Routed experts break the rule on both machines: six positions touch 34.6 distinct experts, not six.

How speculation works. A drafter proposes a block of B − 1 tokens; the target scores all B positions in one pass and keeps the longest prefix that matches its own greedy choice, plus one token of its own. Under greedy decoding the output is identical to plain decoding, token for token; our golden, ISA and RTL keep it bit-exact because every verify position uses the same fixed-order arithmetic as a one-position step (§6.5). The speed-up is the accepted length per pass, τ, divided by the cost of a step relative to a plain step. A step is three phases in series — draft, verify, commit — because the next draft needs the tokens and hidden states the verify accepts, so the drafter's pass sits on the critical path (results/speculative/dflash_step_timing.json). For Qwen3-8B we use DFlash [DFlash], a 1.05 B-parameter block-diffusion drafter that fills a whole block in one parallel pass; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ships its own drafter, DSpark (three stages over a block of five) [DSpark].

Acceptance is a property of the workload

We measured τ on the real Qwen3-8B with the released DFlash drafter on eleven workloads (Table 8-15). The spread is the finding: thinking-mode reasoning, the System-2 use of §2, accepts 3.4–3.9 tokens per pass, less than half the 8.2 we measure on the same MATH-500 prompts with thinking off — which reproduces the paper's 8.01 — and agentic workloads range from 2.6 (web navigation) to 11.3 (JSON). The block matters as much as the workload, so we measure τ directly at each block B, running the reference DFlash generator at that block on the same 264 prompt turns (Table 8-15a), and price every Qwen3-8B speculative figure at the block it runs; the paper's 6.5–8.0 is only a sensitivity. At the ROM design point, block 5, τ is 2.859 pooled over the nine primary workloads (2.33–4.63 by workload); at block 16 it is 3.656. Truncating the block-16 run to B positions agrees with the direct runs within 1.6% pooled at every block but differs by up to 8% on a single workload (Mind2Web at blocks 3 and 4), so the direct runs are the basis. The two machines respond differently

Speculation trades arithmetic for memory traffic: a verify pass does B positions of multiply-accumulates for one read of each weight. An HBM machine decoding one user is bandwidth-bound, so its multipliers idle while weights stream; the extra positions ride on the same read and the pass costs little more than a plain step. A hardwired ROM machine has already removed that bottleneck: each lane consumes one weight per cycle, so a B-position pass costs about B times the lane time and the gain collapses to about τ / B. Lane copies change this: with m copies sharing each weight word (§6.8), a pass costs ⌈B/m⌉ sweeps, and the best block is about m. And on the Qwen3-8B package a second effect appears: the verify slots also share one read of each user's KV cache, so speculation divides the KV stream per token by the tokens per pass — the only lever that takes the package past its KV floor.

Consequences for the design. 8.7 Batching: per-user rate, throughput and energy

Batching does not make a ROM design faster per user, and it fills the two designs differently. The Qwen3-8B package is KV-bound from the second user: its total throughput is flat at 11,921 tokens/s from batch 2, a ceiling the same package on HBM does not reach by 128 users (10,858). The V4.1 array is a 28-stage pipeline whose stages are cut at equal ROM bytes, not equal time: the stage that holds layer 20, whose index scan reads every one of the context's keys, carried 3.4× the mean stage's work at 1M, bound the filled pipeline and cut a user at the 28-user fill to 5,009 tokens/s. The adopted stage rebalancing moves only the scan, whose keys live in HBM, not ROM: from position 400,000 layer 20's keys live on the two stages upstream (S13, S12), which scan their ranges for users past 600K and 900K and return their candidates on the ring (§8.8). Now each user keeps the batch-1 rate up to 28 users at both contexts (7,049 tokens/s at 1M). At saturation the adopted design point reaches 436 K tokens/s at 200K (1,024 users) and 273 K at 1M (the 866 users its HBM holds after the 0.9 capacity reserve), 7.2× and 4.5× the best HBM comparator, whose saturated aggregates are capped at its busiest package link by the same rule (design-point model, results/arch/v41_lanes.json, results/arch/v41_hbm_switched.json; the ROM array is bound by its busiest stage, so the collective tails do not change it), while energy per token falls as the stages fill. With MTP the saturated aggregates are 615 K and 467 K tokens/s (16.7× and 12.6×; the comparator's link caps it at 60% of its uncapped rate); no ROM package link binds, since the busiest stage holds the rate below the head module's link (results/arch/v41_lanes.json energy.*.sat1024_mtp.link_cap). The busiest stage at 1M is now the one holding layer 14's or layer 20's remaining scan; the ratio-2 scans of layers 2, 8 and 14 are split only in MTP verify passes, where their home dies would otherwise exceed the liquid limit.

Where the energy goes. On the Qwen3-8B package at batch 1 (scenario B), 16.7 of the 96.5 mJ per token is in the DRAM stacks and 79.8 mJ on the two dies, 49.2 mJ of which is the dies' share of the HBM path (controller, PHY and I/O) for the 604 MB of FP8 KV; the rest is leakage (9.9), clock (6.1), multiply-accumulates (5.9; 39.7 on the routed lane of scenario A), the KV rings' SRAM traffic (3.3), the ROM read and delivery (2.3), stack idle (2.0), the stream unit (1.0) and the UCIe exchanges (0.01). The KV format is therefore the largest energy lever as well as the largest speed lever, and the ratio to the HBM core moves with the 13.64 pJ/bit HBM path energy, which carries most of the ROM's energy. The same package on HBM at the same weights spends 1,284 mJ, 1,059 mJ of it on its dies: 666 mJ of HBM path for its weights and KV and 383 mJ of leakage, clock and stack idle over a token that takes 1.14 ms. On the V4.1 array the chips' static power, 14.9 kW in the rack model, dominates a batch-1 token; filling the pipeline divides it among 28 users. The hardwired schedule bounds the power. Its saturated worst case has every lane copy multiply-accumulate every cycle, the stream unit and the ROM read path busy every cycle and the stacks at full raw bandwidth; no program can exceed it, so it is provisioned at 1.2× rather than at nameplate. GPUs are provisioned near nameplate because their measured draw can exceed TDP [POLCA, Fan et al.]. The ASAP7 sign-off is power scenario A: its BF16 × BF16 lane at 3.97 pJ per MAC puts each Qwen3-8B die at 1,116 W with DFlash at its design rate (623 W at the calibrated autoregressive rate), capped at 6,680 tokens/s (7,859 without speculation) by the 474.6 W a die of the liquid-cooled package; the production lane of scenario B (0.59 pJ per 8-bit-weight MAC) runs uncapped there, and would cap at 14,176 (8,921 without speculation) with air. The lane must be designed for the ROM's INT8 weights with BF16 activations, operand isolation and clock gating. A GPU tensor core's reported 1.4 pJ per MAC shows that target is not exotic.

8.9 Synchronisation and links against a GB200 NVL72

The order-of-magnitude synchronisation advantage of a compiled schedule is an on-chip property. A dependent handoff costs 5 cycles (4.8 ns) against 371 ns measured on a Blackwell GPU, and a gather from every unit 4.8 ns against about 1 µs: 77× and 208×. Across packages both machines pay SerDes and FEC physics, and the advantage narrows to 7.9–13.4× for a four-die all-reduce against the best measured GB200 kernel and its speed-of-light floor, and 3.6× for a point-to-point stage hop. Per V4.1 token at 1M, synchronisation takes 32.4 µs on the ROM array — collective latency, the collective bytes left exposed under the bench-measured tails, and stage hops — 23% of the token, against 517 µs for the same collective graph at the best GB200 kernel: 16×, or 10× against the floor.

8.10 Design-space exploration: every rejected alternative as an ablation

Every alternative we evaluated either lowered the per-user rate or kept it at the cost of a harder build; none was rejected on taste. In the specification model the largest single effects are the reduction order, the stream-unit width and the engines' widths; the wafer is rejected on aggregate throughput, HBM attach and manufacturing, not on batch-1 latency.

Wafer-scale integration

What it was. The V4.1 package logic laid out as reticle fields stitched on one wafer, so a die-to-die hop becomes an on-wafer field crossing, with KV in HBM stacks around the wafer edge, compared with the array at equal silicon (docs/WAFER_VS_ARRAY_ISO_AREA.md; both priced by the analytical critical-path sensitivity model, the array on shipping two-die packages).

What we found. On a published stitched mesh (125 ns per 28.55 mm field crossing) the wafer is slower at batch 1, 0.81–0.94 of the array, because collectives across its many fields dominate. With a designed express fabric the batch-1 comparison becomes a near tie: the wafer matches the array whenever a crossing costs about 56–71 ns or less, our routed express link crosses a field in about 30 ns, and at 25 ns the wafer leads by 3–11% with the built divider (up to 15% at the wire limit). The wafer loses on aggregate throughput — from three wafers up the array delivers 1.33–1.65× the wafer's aggregate at 4,096 users under every fabric, because at equal silicon it carries 6.5× the HBM stacks — on HBM attach, since a wafer's perimeter gives 43 stacks against 5 per 815 mm² die and must also carry the wafer-to-wafer SerDes, and on manufacturing: in-place defect tolerance instead of discarding bad dies, cross-field stitching, an express long-wire fabric measured only as one link, and whole-wafer test. The manufacturing comparison is qualitative — no yield or cost model exists for either machine — but every item except the array's HBM count per package is harder for the wafer.

9Related work

GPU decode. Serving stacks capture decode in CUDA graphs and fuse element-wise work into neighbouring kernels; Programmatic Dependent Launch overlaps a kernel's prologue with its predecessor. Megakernels run a forward pass as one persistent kernel whose instructions wait on counters in memory: Hazy Research's Llama-1B megakernel [Hazy] and the Mirage Persistent Kernel [MPK]. They remove launch overhead but keep the weight stream (Challenge 1) and the L2 round trips of cross-SM dependencies (Challenge 2), which we measure directly. Vendor-measured concurrency-1 rates [NIM, GPUStack] calibrate our GPU rows; our own measurement shows that batch-1 efficiency against the roofline varies by nearly 2× across stacks (§8.4).

Communication overlap. Fused kernels overlap tensor-parallel communication with computation on GPUs, hiding up to 96% of it for GEMMs [FLUX] and speeding communication-heavy sublayers by 30% on average [T3]; TensorRT-LLM fuses the all-reduce with the following RMSNorm for DeepSeek on B200 [TRT-LLM], and in-fabric reductions cut the latency of small collectives [NVLink SHARP]. We adopt the overlap for the bytes, and find that in decode the latency after the last partial sum remains on the critical path.

Speculative decoding. Draft-and-verify decoding is lossless under greedy and rejection sampling [Leviathan]. Drafters range from feature-level heads (EAGLE-3 [EAGLE-3]) and built-in multi-token prediction (DeepSeek-V3 [DeepSeek-V3], DSpark on DeepSeek-V4 [DSpark]) to block-diffusion drafters that fill a block in one pass (DFlash [DFlash]); serving engines report DSpark's acceptance only as bounds or verify-window lengths [vLLM DSpark, LMSYS DSpark]. All of them were designed for bandwidth-bound GPUs; we show that their benefit depends on the machine's binding resource, that on a ROM machine it returns only with lane copies that share a weight read, and that the acceptance on thinking-mode workloads is half the published figure (§8.6).

On-chip-SRAM dataflow machines. Groq's tensor streaming processor makes execution deterministic by removing reactive hardware (arbiters, caches) and scheduling everything in the compiler [TSP], a principle we share (a compiled schedule verified bit-exactly). Cerebras keeps weights in on-wafer SRAM (vendor-reported 44 GB on 46,225 mm² for WSE-3 [WSE-3]). Both trade capacity for bandwidth with SRAM; ROM's density advantage is what lets a large MoE fit tens of dies rather than many wafers. SambaNova (tiered SRAM/HBM/DDR reconfigurable dataflow), Tenstorrent (mesh of programmable cores) and d-Matrix (SRAM digital in-memory compute) occupy other points of the same trade-off [citation needed].

Model-specific silicon. Etched builds a transformer-specific datapath that keeps weights in HBM [citation needed]. Taalas HC1 hardwires Llama-3.1-8B on one 815 mm² TSMC 6 nm die and reports about 17,000 tokens/s per user [HC1], the closest prior work and our calibration anchor. We differ in targeting a large sparse System-2 model across dies at 200K and 1M context, in keeping per-user state in attached HBM, in designing top-down from a per-token budget with a published arithmetic contract and bit-exact verification chain, and in comparing against the same core on HBM at equal logic area.

Packaging. Two-die packages on interposers of about 3.3 reticles are in volume production; TSMC reports 5.5-reticle CoWoS in production and larger classes to come [TSMC 2026, CoWoS], and the one announced four-die GPU was reportedly cut back to two dies [Rubin Ultra]. Board-reach 112G PAM4 links pay 100 ns or more for KP4 FEC [UCIe PHY, IEEE 802.3df]; the OIF CEI-112G agreements and UALink allow lighter FEC [OIF CEI-112G, UALink], which is the basis of our on-module link; on rack copper IEEE 802.3ck mandates RS(544,514) [802.3ck]. Broadcom's Scale-Up Ethernet budgets under 100 ns for an endpoint's link and PHY and under 250 ns for a switch [SUE], and GB200 all-reduce kernels near their speed-of-light floor take 1.4–2.4 µs [SoL]. HPC interconnects. Special-purpose machines have long bought latency with fixed function: Anton 3 reaches a neighbour node in about 55 ns at 34 ns per hop [Anton 3], Blue Gene/Q's torus about 35 ns per hop [BG/Q], and HPE Slingshot 350 ns per switch [Slingshot]. Our cross-package figures sit in the same range, which is why the synchronisation advantage between packages is modest (§8.9).

10Discussion

10.1 Operating energy and fixed cost

The modeled operating energy is lower at the batch sizes where per-user speed matters. A full cost-per-token result still needs die cost, yield, packaging and amortized design cost, which are not quantified here. The source-pinned reduced-step cross-check gives 5.7× lower energy for ROM weights with the same HBM KV path on both sides; routed logic activity and modeled memory bytes supply that ratio (§8.4). That comparison holds the logic fixed, so it isolates the memory technology; its absolute values are ASAP7-predictive. The specification models extend it to the design points under assumed production energy inputs (Table 10-1). On the power scenarios' inputs, at batch 1 the Qwen3-8B package spends 96.5 mJ per token against 1,284 mJ for the same package on HBM at the same 8-bit weights on the production lane (13.3×; 130 against 1,318 mJ, 10.1×, on our routed lane); most of the ROM's energy is its own KV stream, so the ratio is sensitive to the HBM energy assumption. The adopted V4.1 design at 1M, priced with the best HBM comparator on one power model (logic dies and their HBM stacks, static power, the always-on links and every user's KV reads from HBM charged to both), uses 2.8× less energy per token than the comparator at batch 1 (2.40 against 6.70 J; 3.0× with MTP), where both are static-dominated, and 6.8× less at the 28-user pipeline fill (151 against 1,023 mJ; 9.3× with MTP); at 200K the same points are 2.9×, 3.2×, 7.8× and 11.2× (results/arch/power_scenarios.json rom_over_hbm, scenario B, design-point rates with the bench-measured collective tails). The comparator's modelled wall power per package, 0.60–0.97 kW with its switch share, sits within the range of reported B200 power figures. The fill figure is the energy headline. The advantage narrows with batch on Qwen3-8B, because per-user KV traffic, not weights, dominates both machines' energy (96.5 against 110.5 mJ at 128 users on the production lane, §8.7), and it holds on V4.1, where the array's energy per token falls with batch as well. Power provisioning favours the fixed schedule too: its saturated worst case bounds its draw, so the V4.1 rack is provisioned at 39.9 kW against 132 kW for a GB200 NVL72 (§8.8).

Throughput per silicon is not the price of speed either. The Qwen3-8B package's total throughput from two users on (11,921 tokens/s, KV-bound) is above what the same package on HBM reaches at 128 users (10,858), and the adopted V4.1 design's aggregate is 5.8× the best HBM array's at the 28-user fill at 1M (10.6× with MTP, where the comparator's busiest package link caps it); the adopted scan rebalance keeps each ROM user at the batch-1 rate through the fill. The energy comparisons use the production-value power inputs in §8.8.

The fixed cost includes the mask set and design. Leading-edge mask sets are estimated in the tens of millions of dollars and complete designs at 7 nm at $50–75 M [SemiAnalysis]. Because the ROM is via-programmed, a new model or version changes the via layers and the ROM contents, not the logic (docs/MEMORY_COMPILERS_AND_BIST.md), so the per-model cost is a partial mask set [citation needed for its price] on an otherwise reused design. The fixed cost is amortised over the tokens served in the model's deployment [citation needed for typical volumes], and latency has value of its own for the workloads of Table 4-1, which a cost-per-token metric does not capture.

10.2 Why build silicon for one model?

Three observations make a model-specific chip reasonable. First, the paired System-1/System-2 structure separates a slowly changing interaction layer from a replaceable background model; OpenAI describes the background model of GPT-Live as swappable without replacing the real-time layer [GPT-Live], so the model that most needs speed is also the one that changes least. Second, frontier open architectures are converging: latent or compressed KV, learned sparse attention and mixtures of experts recur across successive generations [DS-V2, DS-V3.2, DS-V4], so the units a model like V4.1 needs are likely to be needed again. Third, only the ROM contents are model-specific: the vector stream unit and arithmetic contract are shared between the Qwen3 and V4.1 cores, and the unit library, the top-down budget tools, the verification chain and the physical flow carry over.

The costs are real. A model cannot be updated in the field; a new version needs new via masks and a new die run; capacity is fixed at fabrication; and a model whose value lies in frequent retraining is a poor fit. Deployment lifetimes of production models are not well documented publicly [citation needed], and the economics of §10.1 depend on them.

10.3 Best achievable today, and what comes after

The adopted V4.1 array uses validated package and rack links, in-switch reduction and four HBM3E stacks per die. The next gains depend on measured integrated behavior: closing the 1.087 GHz whole-core route, recovering the rest of the collective overlap (C7: 7,049 against 7,374 tokens/s at 1M with the adopted relay, 4-word gathers, 3-cycle fold adders, partial stage-hop splits and hc_post early start; the hop split's package-link cost at batch > 1 is unmeasured; a reduced real-core all-reduce stage is exact at both rack delays, and the next C7 step is a streaming real-core consumer and the gather and hop classes with real producers), and reducing the remaining package collective latency. If silicon misses the latency target, the area envelope allows wider attention, index and weight engines, but their full-core benefit must be remeasured. For Qwen3-8B at 8K, the FP8 KV stream is the rate limit and lane energy is the power limit; changes to either require a new accuracy or physical result.

10.4 Limitations

The headline rates are models, not silicon. The Qwen3-8B headline is the DFlash serial step on the specification chain; it is not yet re-priced on the calibrated core. Its acceptance is measured in BF16 (the deployed 8-bit weights meet the quality threshold, but acceptance under them is not measured), its RTL token gates reach 2,048 positions, not 8K, and they run one die with BF16 weights, not the two-die tensor-parallel split with INT8 weights. The autoregressive Qwen3-8B rate beneath it is the RTL-calibrated timing model — fitted within a few cycles to the RTL on the reduced vehicle — run at the specification's widths on the real program, and it assumes a design-shape FP8 KV path whose integrated vector reads and writes pass a functional RTL sequence (18 exact steps from an empty cache on physical-HBM KV) on a one-cycle behavioural HBM and again on a timed four-pseudo-channel controller model (12% more core cycles on the reduced vehicle), so its physical bandwidth and refresh timing are not yet calibrated; the V4.1 headline is a design-point model with bench-measured collective tails and modelled on-die wire, not measured chip throughput, with the adopted collective levers measured bit-exact in an RTL stage bench and the on-die wire priced on the layer die's analytical floorplan, and the specification model beneath it is verified on the program by an event replay, with independent attention and vector benches, the combined reduced pooled-indexer and QE weight-HBM gate, and the whole-core route still pending. The clocks are the slowest routed units, not closed cores. The per-user rates are calibrated only against one self-reported product; memory macros are model-grade views on a predictive PDK; the GPU boundary costs are measured on a workstation Blackwell (GB202), not a B200; Qwen3-8B's DFlash acceptance is measured in BF16 on a GPU, not in the deployed arithmetic, and below block 16 with the block-16-trained drafter outside its training distribution; and V4.1's acceptance length (τ = 3.65) is measured on V4.1-Flash with greedy decoding and its built-in drafter, not with sampling or in the deployed arithmetic (published V4.1-Flash figures span 3.5–4.1). ROM removes weight traffic, not per-user state traffic, so models whose state grows with context (Table 3-1) gain less, and at long context the ROM ÷ HBM advantage shrinks (Table 8-11).

11Conclusion

Fast per-user decode is limited on today's accelerators by weight bandwidth, by the synchronisation of a long dependency chain, and at long context by the bandwidth of each user's KV cache. Fixing the model in silicon removes the first and turns the second into a design problem: mask ROM beside the lanes makes weight bandwidth a function of area, and a compiled schedule of fixed-latency units turns on-chip synchronisation into a few cycles, while between packages it leaves only fixed-function link crossings — but only if every unit is built to a per-token budget. Designing top-down from that budget, with an arithmetic contract chosen for hardware and per-user state in attached HBM, one Qwen3-8B two-reticle package, every layer split across its two dies, decoding with DFlash reaches an 18,720-token/s design rate at 8K, uncapped on the production lane in a liquid-cooled package (6,680 with the lane as built). This rests on BF16 acceptance; its 8-bit weights meet the quality threshold in the deployment arithmetic. Its calibrated autoregressive chain reaches 10,968 tokens/s, 92% of the ideal KV bandwidth ceiling of its eight stacks. The DeepSeek-V4.1 one-rack design projects 7,049 tokens/s per user at 1M (15,890 with native speculation), a design-point model with bench-measured collective tails and modelled on-die wire, not measured chip throughput — 7,374 if the collectives fully overlapped, which they do not yet, and 8,246 without the on-die wire the layer die's floorplan costs — conditional on the 1.087 GHz system clock, not yet demonstrated on the whole system: 2.0× an HBM array of equal logic area in its best configuration on shipping hardware (1.8× when both speculate), with 6.8–9.3× less energy per token at a filled pipeline on one power model for both machines, and a rack provisioned at 39.9 kW, about 29% of an NVL72, whose hottest die remains within the liquid limit at every modeled point after the scan rebalance, all on link and power values checked against their standards. What remains to settle is the whole-core simulation and routing of the adopted cores, the FP8 KV streamer, a multiply-accumulate lane within the power budget, and the accuracy of the quantised formats; each is marked pending with the record that will settle it.
